\begin{abstract}
We provide the algorithmic and computational complement to the theoretical framework of~\cite{Piel.2026} for hierarchical subdivision $k$-form spaces on triangular meshes. The central observation is that every basis function is represented by a column of the accumulated subdivision matrix. Selecting active degrees of freedom results in collecting their corresponding columns into the hierarchical subdivision matrix. This sparse matrix reduces all downstream operations like matrix assembly or residual evaluation to standard sparse linear algebra on the finest mesh $\T_\L$ without recomputing any integrals. The substantive question is which selections of active degrees of freedom are admissible, in particular linearly independent and exactness of the complex. We answer this question via purely combinatorial admissibility conditions. We provide explicit algorithms for constructing the active degrees of freedom from a domain sequence, obtained level by level by adding fine degrees of freedom and removing linearly dependent coarse ones.

For the adaptive solution of PDEs, we describe a \textsc{Solve}--\textsc{Estimate}--\textsc{Mark}--\textsc{Refine}--\textsc{Pad} loop in which the non-standard \textsc{Pad} step enforces the admissible domain sequence conditions after Dörfler marking by applying pinch resolution, inadmissible-faces padding, and grading, refining the coarse regions that would otherwise break admissibility. Numerical experiments on the Maxwell eigenvalue problem confirm that exact admissible domain sequences reproduce the reference spectrum without spurious modes and adaptive experiments on the mixed vector Laplace equation demonstrate the adaptive loop.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

The finite element exterior calculus (FEEC)~\cite{Arnold.FEEC} provides a systematic framework for constructing stable, structure-preserving discretisations of partial differential equations (PDEs) involving differential forms. Its central structure is the discrete de Rham complex: a sequence of finite-dimensional function spaces connected by the exterior derivative $\extd$. Exactness of this complex is the key algebraic property that prevents spurious modes in Maxwell eigenvalue problems, guarantees inf-sup stability in mixed formulations, and underpins the commuting diagram that makes error analysis tractable.\needcite{} Classical realisations of the FEEC use the lowest-order Whitney forms, also known as the continuous Galerkin ($\mathrm{CG}_1$), N\'ed\'elec ($\mathrm{NED}_1$), and discontinuous ($\mathrm{DG}_0$) spaces.\needcite{}

Subdivision algorithms, originally developed for geometric modelling, produce limit functions that are globally smooth on arbitrary triangular meshes.\needcite{} For example, Loop's scheme~\citep{Loop.1987} generates globally $\mathcal{C}^1$ scalar limit functions. Wang~\citep{Wang.2006,Wang.2008} introduce subdivision schemes for $1$- and $2$-forms that commute with the discrete exterior derivative from Discrete Exterior Calculus (DEC, \cite{Hirani.2003,Desbrun.2006}). These subdivision schemes give rise to subdivision $k$-form spaces in the DEC sense \citep{Goes.2015}. \cite{Piel.2026} translate their work to finite elements and introduce a framework for subdivision-based finite elements that form discrete de Rham complexes on meshes with uniform refinement.

Such uniform subdivision spaces assign the same resolution everywhere: every basis function has a support whose size is determined by the coarse mesh $\T_\lzero$. This is adequate for globally smooth solutions but wasteful when the PDE solution is rough only in a small part of the domain. In isogeometric analysis, the analogous limitation of globally refined B-spline spaces has been addressed through hierarchical B-spline spaces~\citep{Evans.2020}, which mix basis functions from different refinement levels to obtain spatially varying resolution while retaining linear independence. Extending this idea to spaces of differential forms is non-trivial: activating basis functions at different levels can break the exactness of the de Rham complex unless the selection is governed by a compatible combinatorial rule.
The analogous construction for B-spline de Rham complexes has been studied in~\cite{Buffa2011,Evans.2020,Shepherd.2024,Dijkstra.2025}, where admissibility conditions on the hierarchical mesh ensure exactness of the complex.
A central mechanism in these works is a \emph{completion step} that, after marking basis functions for refinement, enforces a compatibility condition on the active set: in~\cite{Cabanas.2026} this takes the form of \emph{$L$-chain completion}, which forces all basis functions in a connecting $L$-chain to be refined simultaneously whenever a conflicting pair is detected.

This paper provides the matrix-level description and algorithms for hierarchical subdivision $k$-form spaces that preserve the de Rham complex, serving as the practical and computational complement to~\cite{companion_paper}. The key computational objects are the subdivision matrices $\Amat{k}{\l}{\L}$, which represent level-$\l$ basis functions in the Whitney basis of the fine assembly mesh $\T_\L$. A hierarchical space is formed by selecting one column of these matrices per active basis function, according to a \emph{admissible domain sequence} of $\T_\L$, a combinatorial condition on the patch decomposition of the domain. This condition, characterised in~\cite{companion_paper}, simultaneously guarantees linear independence and exactness of the complex. Once the hierarchical subdivision matrix $\Amat{k}{\activeDoFs}{\L}$ is assembled, all downstream operations (exterior derivative, mass and stiffness matrices, residual evaluation) reduce to standard sparse linear algebra on $\T_\L$ without recomputing any integrals.

\paragraph*{Contributions.} This paper makes the following contributions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item A matrix-level construction of hierarchical subdivision $k$-form spaces (Section~\ref{subsec:computational_representations}),
    \item A selection procedure for the active degrees of freedom and a practical algorithm for assembling the hierarchical subdivision matrix (Sections~\ref{subsec:selecting_active_dofs} and~\ref{subsec:implementation_active_dofs}),
    \item An adaptive refinement loop \textsc{Solve}--\textsc{Estimate}--\textsc{Mark}--\textsc{Refine}--\textsc{Pad}, including a vertex-based marking and refinement scheme and a non-standard padding step that restores the admissibility conditions after refinement (Section~\ref{subsec:adaptive_loop}),
    \item Numerical verification via atomic exactness test cases and the Maxwell eigenvalue problem to confirm the exactness of the discrete complex, and adaptive simulation of the vector Laplace equation to confirm admissibility across the adaptive loop (Section~\ref{sec:numerical_experiments}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Subdivision $k$-form spaces: from uniform to hierarchical}

\subsection{Subdivision basis functions}

This section collects the most important properties of the uniform subdivision $k$-form spaces introduced in~\cite{Piel.2026}. We focus on the aspects that enable the construction of hierarchical subdivision $k$-form spaces in a similar way as~\cite{Evans.2020} did for B-splines.

Starting from an initial coarse triangular mesh $\T_\l$ at level $\l = \lzero$, the \emph{subdivision algorithm} produces a sequence of increasingly fine meshes $\T_{\lzero+1}, \ldots, \T_\L$ by repeatedly applying two steps: a \emph{topological refinement} that splits every triangle into four, and a \emph{geometric averaging} step that repositions vertices as weighted averages of neighbouring coarse vertices. We use Loop's subdivision scheme~\cite{Loop.1987} throughout. The resulting \emph{mesh hierarchy} $\{\T_\l\}_{\l=\lzero}^{\L}$ ranges from the coarsest level $\lzero$ to the finest mesh $\T_\L$. At each level $\l$, we denote the sets of vertices, edges, and faces by $\K^0_\l = \V_\l$, $\K^1_\l = \E_\l$, and $\K^2_\l = \F_\l$, with cardinalities $|\K^k_\l|$.

On each mesh $\T_\l$ along the mesh hierarchy, the lowest-order Whitney $k$-form spaces are the standard FEEC spaces~\citep{Arnold.2006}
\begin{equation}
    \FEECspace{0}{\l} = \mathrm{CG}_1(\T_\l), \qquad \FEECspace{1}{\l} = \mathrm{RT}_0(\T_\l), \qquad \FEECspace{2}{\l} = \mathrm{DG}_0(\T_\l),
\end{equation}
with basis functions $\freeQ{\FEECbasis}{k}{\l}{\coarseIdxone}$ indexed by the $k$-simplices $\simplex{k}{\l}{\coarseIdxone} \in \K^k_\l$.

The subdivision algorithm induces a linear \emph{subdivision operator} $\Sop{k}{\l}{\l+1}: \FEECspace{k}{\l} \to \FEECspace{k}{\l+1}$ between Whitney spaces on consecutive levels. Composing these one-step operators gives the \emph{accumulated subdivision operator}
\begin{equation}
    \Aop{k}{\l}{\L} \coloneqq \Sop{k}{\L-1}{\L} \circ \cdots \circ \Sop{k}{\l}{\l+1},
\end{equation}
whose coordinate representation with respect to the Whitney bases of $\FEECspace{k}{\l}$ and $\FEECspace{k}{\L}$ is the \emph{subdivision matrix} $\Amat{k}{\l}{\L}$ of size $|\K^k_\L| \times |\K^k_\l|$. Column $\coarseIdxone$ of $\Amat{k}{\l}{\L}$ is the coordinate vector of the image of $\freeQ{\FEECbasis}{k}{\l}{\coarseIdxone}$ in the fine Whitney basis of $\FEECspace{k}{\L}$.

The \emph{subdivision $k$-form space} on level $\l$ is defined as the image of $\FEECspace{k}{\l}$ under $\Aop{k}{\l}{\L}$,
\begin{equation}
    \label{def:uniform_spaces_as_image_of_subdiv_operator}
    \SDFspace{k}{\l}{\L}\big(\T_\l\big) \coloneqq \Aop{k}{\l}{\L}\!\big(\FEECspace{k}{\l}\big),
\end{equation}
spanned by the \emph{subdivision basis functions} $\constrQ{\SDFbasis}{k}{\l}{\L}{\coarseIdxone} \coloneqq \Aop{k}{\l}{\L}\, \freeQ{\FEECbasis}{k}{\l}{\coarseIdxone}$, i.e.\ the columns of $\Amat{k}{\l}{\L}$ expressed in the fine Whitney basis. At the finest level, $\SDFspace{k}{\L}{\L} = \FEECspace{k}{\L}$ by definition. The key properties of the spaces $\SDFspace{k}{\l}{\L}$ were established in~\cite{Piel.2026} and will be recalled in Section~\ref{sec:construction_of_hierarchical_subdiv_spaces}.

\subsection{Computational representations}
\label{subsec:computational_representations}

This section establishes the computational representation of uniform subdivision $k$-form spaces and how it extends to mixed-level representations spanned by basis functions across multiple levels. The resulting framework is fixed for the remainder of this work, and Table~\ref{tab:properties_map} identifies the properties we need to ensure for the spanned spaces.

\subsubsection{Uniform representation}
\label{subsubsec:uniform_representation}

A $k$-form $\constrVec{\omega}{k}{\l}{\L} \in \SDFspace{k}{\l}{\L}$ can be written as
\begin{equation}
    \constrVec{\omega}{k}{\l}{\L} = \sum\limits_{\coarseIdxone = 1}^{\sumdimlk{\l}{k}} \freeQ{c}{k}{\l}{\coarseIdxone} \constrQ{\SDFbasis}{k}{\l}{\L}{\coarseIdxone} = \sum\limits_{i = 1}^{\sumdimlk{\l}{k}} \freeQ{c}{k}{\l}{\coarseIdxone} \; \Big( \sum\limits_{\fineIdxone = 1}^{\sumdimlk{\L}{k}} \Amatij{k}{\l}{\L}{\fineIdxone}{\coarseIdxone} \freeQ{\FEECbasis}{k}{\L}{\fineIdxone} \Big).
\end{equation}
We can vectorise this equation by introducing the vectors
\begin{equation}
    \freeVec{\bar{c}}{k}{\l} \coloneqq \begin{bmatrix}
        \freeQ{c}{k}{\l}{1} \\
        \freeQ{c}{k}{\l}{2} \\
        \vdots \\
        \freeQ{c}{k}{\l}{\sumdimlk{\l}{k}}
    \end{bmatrix}, \qquad 
    \constrVec{\SDFbases}{k}{\l}{\L} \coloneqq \begin{bmatrix}
        \constrQ{\SDFbasis}{k}{\l}{\L}{1} \\
        \constrQ{\SDFbasis}{k}{\l}{\L}{2} \\
        \vdots \\
        \constrQ{\SDFbasis}{k}{\l}{\L}{\sumdimlk{\l}{k}}
    \end{bmatrix}, \qquad 
    \freeVec{\FEECbases}{k}{\L} \coloneqq \begin{bmatrix}
        \freeQ{\FEECbasis}{k}{\L}{1} \\
        \freeQ{\FEECbasis}{k}{\L}{2} \\
        \vdots \\
        \freeQ{\FEECbasis}{k}{\L}{\sumdimlk{\L}{k}}
    \end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}
We can then re-write $\constrVec{\omega}{k}{\l}{\L} \in \SDFspace{k}{\l}{\L}$ as
\begin{equation}
    \constrVec{\omega}{k}{\l}{\L} = \big( \constrVec{\SDFbases}{k}{\l}{\L} \big)^\top \cdot \freeVec{\bar{c}}{k}{\l} = \big( \freeVec{\FEECbases}{k}{\L} \big)^\top \cdot \Amat{k}{\l}{\L} \cdot \freeVec{\bar{c}}{k}{\l}.
\end{equation}
This implies
\begin{equation}
    \big( \freeVec{\FEECbases}{k}{\L} \big)^\top \cdot \Amat{k}{\l}{\L} = \big( \constrVec{\SDFbases}{k}{\l}{\L} \big)^\top = \begin{bmatrix}
        \constrQ{\SDFbasis}{k}{\l}{\L}{1} & \constrQ{\SDFbasis}{k}{\l}{\L}{2} & \dots & \constrQ{\SDFbasis}{k}{\l}{\L}{\sumdimlk{\l}{k}}
    \end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}
and thus shows that the columns of the subdivision matrix $\Amat{k}{\l}{\L}$ contain the coefficients of the subdivision basis functions $\constrQ{\SDFbasis}{k}{\l}{\L}{\coarseIdxone}$ in the fine FEEC space $\FEECspace{k}{\L}$.

\subsubsection{Mixed-level representation}
\label{subsubsec:hierarchical_representation}

Uniform subdivision spaces $\SDFspace{k}{\l}{\L}$ resolve the domain at a single global scale, which is wasteful when the PDE solution contains localised features. We now construct spaces that break this uniformity by mixing basis functions from different levels $\l$. For example, a level-$0$ basis function $\constrQ{\SDFbasis}{k}{\lzero}{\L}{\coarseIdxone}$ corresponding to $\simplex{k}{\lzero}{\coarseIdxone}$ has broad support capturing large-scale variation, while a level-$2$ basis function $\constrQ{\SDFbasis}{k}{2}{\L}{\coarseIdxone}$ corresponding to $\simplex{k}{2}{\coarseIdxone}$ has a much smaller support and can resolve local detail. We refer to the selection of simplices as the \emph{active DoFs} and denote them by $\activeDoFSet{k}{}$, while $\activeDoFSet{k}{\l}$ denotes all active DoFs on level $\l$. The \emph{active basis functions} are denoted by
\begin{equation}
    \constrVec{\SDFbases}{k}{\activeDoFSet{}{}}{\L} \coloneq \bigcup\limits_{\l = \lzero}^\L \; \bigcup\limits_{\simplex{k}{\l}{\coarseIdxone} \in \activeDoFSet{k}{\l}} \; \constrQ{\SDFbasis}{k}{\l}{\L}{\coarseIdxone}.
\end{equation}

The representation of Section~\ref{subsubsec:uniform_representation} extends to the active DoFs $\activeDoFSet{k}{}$. A mixed-level form $\constrVec{\omega}{k}{\activeDoFs}{\L} \in \mathrm{span}(\constrVec{\SDFbases}{k}{\activeDoFSet{}{}}{\L})$ is a linear combination of the active basis functions drawn from all levels, i.e.
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:hierarchical_form_sum}
    \constrVec{\omega}{k}{\activeDoFs}{\L}
    = \sum\limits_{\l=\lzero}^{\L} \; \sum\limits_{\simplex{k}{\l}{\coarseIdxone} \in \activeDoFSet{k}{\l}}
        \freeQ{c}{k}{\l}{\coarseIdxone} \, \constrQ{\SDFbasis}{k}{\l}{\L}{\coarseIdxone}.
\end{equation}
The active DoFs use the same simplex indices $\coarseIdxone$ as in the uniform space $\SDFspace{k}{\l}{\L}$, but restricted to $\activeDoFSet{k}{\l} \subset \K^k_\l$. To form explicit vectors we enumerate the active simplices on level $\l$ in increasing index order as $\simplex{k}{\l}{\coarseIdxone_1}, \ldots, \simplex{k}{\l}{\coarseIdxone_{|\activeDoFSet{k}{\l}|}}$ with $\coarseIdxone_1 < \cdots < \coarseIdxone_{|\activeDoFSet{k}{\l}|}$. The concatenated basis-function and coefficient vectors, grouped by level with blocks separated by vertical bars, are
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:hierarchical_dof_vector}
    \big(\freeVec{\bar{c}}{k}{\activeDoFs}\big)^\top
    = \Bigl[\;
        \underbrace{
            \freeQ{c}{k}{0}{\coarseIdxone_1}
            \;\; \cdots \;\;
            \freeQ{c}{k}{0}{\coarseIdxone_{|\activeDoFSet{k}{0}|}}
        }_{\displaystyle (\freeVec{\bar{c}}{k}{0})^\top}
        \;\Big|\;
        \underbrace{
            \freeQ{c}{k}{1}{\coarseIdxone_1}
            \;\; \cdots \;\;
            \freeQ{c}{k}{1}{\coarseIdxone_{|\activeDoFSet{k}{1}|}}
        }_{\displaystyle (\freeVec{\bar{c}}{k}{1})^\top}
        \;\Big|\;
        \cdots
        \;\Big|\;
        \underbrace{
            \freeQ{c}{k}{\L}{\coarseIdxone_1}
            \;\; \cdots \;\;
            \freeQ{c}{k}{\L}{\coarseIdxone_{|\activeDoFSet{k}{\L}|}}
        }_{\displaystyle (\freeVec{\bar{c}}{k}{\L})^\top}
    \;\Bigr],
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:hierarchical_basis_vector}
    \constrVec{\SDFbases}{k}{\activeDoFs}{\L}
    = \Bigl[\;
        \underbrace{
            \constrQ{\SDFbasis}{k}{0}{\L}{\coarseIdxone_1}
            \;\cdots\;
            \constrQ{\SDFbasis}{k}{0}{\L}{\coarseIdxone_{|\activeDoFSet{k}{0}|}}
        }_{\activeDoFSet{k}{0}}
        \;\Big|\;
        \underbrace{
            \constrQ{\SDFbasis}{k}{1}{\L}{\coarseIdxone_1}
            \;\cdots\;
            \constrQ{\SDFbasis}{k}{1}{\L}{\coarseIdxone_{|\activeDoFSet{k}{1}|}}
        }_{\activeDoFSet{k}{1}}
        \;\Big|\;
        \cdots
        \;\Big|\;
        \underbrace{
            \constrQ{\SDFbasis}{k}{\L}{\L}{\coarseIdxone_1}
            \;\cdots\;
            \constrQ{\SDFbasis}{k}{\L}{\L}{\coarseIdxone_{|\activeDoFSet{k}{\L}|}}
        }_{\activeDoFSet{k}{\L}}
    \;\Bigr].
\end{equation}
With the active subdivision basis $\constrVec{\SDFbases}{k}{\activeDoFs}{\L}$ chosen and the fine FEEC basis $\freeVec{\FEECbases}{k}{\L}$ fixed, the form $\constrVec{\omega}{k}{\activeDoFs}{\L}$ admits two representations that must agree. Equating them and comparing coefficients in $\FEECspace{k}{\L}$ defines the hierarchical subdivision matrix $\Amat{k}{\activeDoFs}{\L}$:
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:hierarchical_form_compact}
    \constrVec{\omega}{k}{\activeDoFs}{\L}
    = \big(\constrVec{\SDFbases}{k}{\activeDoFs}{\L}\big)^\top \cdot \freeVec{\bar{c}}{k}{\activeDoFs}
    = \big(\freeVec{\FEECbases}{k}{\L}\big)^\top \cdot \Amat{k}{\activeDoFs}{\L} \cdot \freeVec{\bar{c}}{k}{\activeDoFs}.
\end{equation}
This implies $\big(\freeVec{\FEECbases}{k}{\L}\big)^\top \cdot \Amat{k}{\activeDoFs}{\L} = \big(\constrVec{\SDFbases}{k}{\activeDoFs}{\L}\big)^\top$, showing that the columns of $\Amat{k}{\activeDoFs}{\L}$ are the coefficients of the active subdivision basis functions in $\FEECspace{k}{\L}$. The level grouping of $\freeVec{\bar{c}}{k}{\activeDoFs}$ mirrors the block column structure of $\Amat{k}{\activeDoFs}{\L}$:
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:hierarchical_matrix_blocks}
    \Amat{k}{\activeDoFs}{\L}
    =
    \Bigl[
        \underbrace{\bigl(\Amat{k}{0}{\L}\bigr)_{\activeDoFSet{k}{0}}}_{\in \mathbb{R}^{|\K^k_\L| \times |\activeDoFSet{k}{0}|}}
        \;\Big|\;
        \underbrace{\bigl(\Amat{k}{1}{\L}\bigr)_{\activeDoFSet{k}{1}}}_{\in \mathbb{R}^{|\K^k_\L| \times |\activeDoFSet{k}{1}|}}
        \;\Big|\;
        \cdots
        \;\Big|\;
        \underbrace{\bigl(\Amat{k}{\L}{\L}\bigr)_{\activeDoFSet{k}{\L}}}_{\in \mathbb{R}^{|\K^k_\L| \times |\activeDoFSet{k}{\L}|}}
    \Bigr],
\end{equation}
so that the block $(\freeVec{\bar{c}}{k}{\l})^\top$ multiplies precisely the $|\activeDoFSet{k}{\l}|$ columns of $\Amat{k}{\activeDoFs}{\L}$ originating from level $\l$, contributing $\sum_{\simplex{k}{\l}{\coarseIdxone} \in \activeDoFSet{k}{\l}} \freeQ{c}{k}{\l}{\coarseIdxone} \, \constrQ{\SDFbasis}{k}{\l}{\L}{\coarseIdxone}$ to $\constrVec{\omega}{k}{\activeDoFs}{\L}$.

\smallskip

From here the matrix picture is fixed. The object $\Amat{k}{\activeDoFs}{\L}$ is always a selection of columns from the accumulated subdivision matrices $\Amat{k}{\l}{\L}$ for all $\l \in \{\lzero, \, \dots, \, \L\}$. The remaining task is thus to select the active DoFs $\activeDoFSet{k}{}$ to enable the concrete construction of $\Amat{k}{\activeDoFs}{\L}$. The following section shows that designing a selection algorithm that guarantees desirable properties for the spanned spaces turns out to be a subtle matter.

\subsection{Unrefinement, the solve, and the case for admissibility}
\label{subsec:unrefinement_and_solve}

This section provides a high-level overview of the assembly of a linear form into a linear system using the active DoFs $\activeDoFSet{k}{}$, and how to solve this system. Every active basis function $\constrQ{\SDFbasis}{k}{\l}{\L}{\coarseIdxone} \in \activeDoFSet{k}{}$ is an element of the finest FEEC space $\FEECspace{k}{\L}$, and we use this inclusion to assemble matrices for the active DoFs. Given we assembled a linear form into the matrix $M_\L$ with respect to the FEEC spaces $\FEECspace{k}{\L}$, the corresponding matrix $M_{\activeDoFSet{k}{}}$ is given by
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:unrefinement}
    M_{\activeDoFs^k} \; \coloneq \; \big( \Amat{k}{\activeDoFs}{\L} \big)^{\!\top} \, \cdot \, M_\L \, \cdot \, \Amat{k}{\activeDoFs}{\L}.
\end{equation}

We call this restriction onto the active DoFs $\activeDoFSet{k}{}$ the \emph{unrefinement}. The right-hand side $b_{\activeDoFSet{k}{}}$ of the linear form is obtained in a similar way. Example~\ref{ex:matrix_assembly} spells out the unrefinement for the mixed Laplace system~\eqref{eq:mixed_block_system} used to test our algorithms later. The resulting linear system is given by
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:abstract_solve}
    M_{\activeDoFSet{k}{}} \, \freeVec{\bar{c}}{k}{\activeDoFs} = b_{\activeDoFSet{k}{}}
\end{equation}
for the adaptive coefficient vector $\freeVec{\bar{c}}{k}{\activeDoFs}$ as in Equation~\eqref{eq:hierarchical_form_compact}. This system is well posed iff the matrix $M_{\activeDoFSet{k}{}}$ has full rank, and thus no kernel. Assuming that the problem is well posed in $\FEECspace{k}{\L}$, the matrix $M_\L$ has full rank. This means that a kernel of $M_{\activeDoFSet{k}{}}$ has to enter through a kernel in $\Amat{k}{\activeDoFs}{\L}$. If $\Amat{k}{\activeDoFs}{\L}$ has no non-trivial kernel, we call the selection of active DoFs \emph{admissible}.

\smallskip

There are two cases that cause inadmissibility because $\Amat{k}{\activeDoFs}{\L}$ picks up a kernel. First, multiple active DoFs in $\activeDoFSet{k}{}$ can be linearly dependent, and thus introduce a kernel into $\Amat{k}{\activeDoFs}{\L}$. Second, even if all active DoFs are linearly independent, $\Amat{k}{\activeDoFs}{\L}$ can carry a spurious kernel by inflating the space of harmonic forms in the differential complex beyond the true harmonics.

Controlling both linear independence and the cohomology of the discrete complex spanned by the active DoFs $\activeDoFSet{k}{}$ implies well-posedness of the linear system in Equation~\eqref{eq:abstract_solve}. Section~\ref{sec:construction_of_hierarchical_subdiv_spaces} introduces the necessary tools and operators to enable a systematic construction of admissible active DoFs, and Section~\ref{subsec:adaptive_loop} extends that to a series of nested spaces to enable adaptive, local refinement. The resulting algorithms are verified numerically in Section~\ref{sec:numerical_experiments}. Table~\ref{tab:properties_map} maps the desirable properties of the spaces and algorithms to where they are studied in the remainder of this paper. The table provides the structure of the presentation, clarifies the claims and serves as an ongoing reference as this paper is read.

\section{Constructing hierarchical subdivision spaces}
\label{sec:construction_of_hierarchical_subdiv_spaces}

The general matrix representation of Section~\ref{subsec:computational_representations} is unchanged in the following. This section presents the selection of admissible active DoFs. We introduce refinement and derivative stencils as visual tools to understand linear independence and the inclusion of differentials of $k$-forms in the $k\!+\!1$-form space. The section ends by introducing admissible domain sequences, an object that parameterises the DoF selection and enforces admissibility through geometric constraints regarding the decomposition of the domain mesh into patches of different subdivision levels.

\subsection{Refinement of basis functions}
\label{subsec:refinement_of_basis_functions}

\begin{proposition}[Proposition~3.13 from~\cite{Piel.2026}]
\label{prop:uniform_spaces_nested}
Let $\lzero \leq \l \leq \L$. For a fixed initial meshes $\T_{\l}$ for $\l \in \{\lzero, \hdots, \L\}$, the
corresponding subdivision $k$-form spaces $\SDFspace{k}{\l}{\L}\big(\T_{\l} \big)$ are nested, i.e.
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:nested_spaces}
    \SDFspace{k}{\l}{\L} \subset  \SDFspace{k}{\l+1}{\L} \subset \hdots \subset \SDFspace{k}{\L}{\L} = \FEECspace{k}{\L},
\end{equation}
and have the dimensions $\dimlk{\l}{k} < \dimlk{\l +1}{k} < \hdots < \dimlk{\L}{k}$.
\end{proposition}

This proposition implies the refinement relation for the subdivision basis functions $\constrQ{\SDFbasis}{k}{\l}{\L}{\coarseIdxone}$. The resulting properties regarding the refinability of the basis functions form the backbone of the adaptive method presented in this paper. The following discussion is focused on basis functions on intermediate levels $\l$ with $\lzero \leq \l \leq \L$ defined as
\begin{equation}
    \label{equ:basisrel}
    \constrQ{\SDFbasis}{k}{\l}{\L}{\coarseIdxone} = \Aop{k}{\l}{\L} \freeQ{\FEECbasis}{k}{\l}{\coarseIdxone} = \sum_{\fineIdxone = 1}^{\sumdimlk{\l}{k}} \Amatij{k}{\l}{\L}{\fineIdxone}{\coarseIdxone} \; \freeQ{\FEECbasis}{k}{\L}{\fineIdxone} \, ,
\end{equation}
where $\Amat{k}{\l}{\L}$ is the subdivision matrix, cf. Equation~\ref{def:uniform_spaces_as_image_of_subdiv_operator} and~\cite{Piel.2026}. Splitting the accumulated subdivision operator $\Aop{k}{\l}{\L}$ reveals the following relation between the basis functions $\constrVec{\Phi}{k}{\l}{\L}$ and $\constrVec{\Phi}{k}{\l + 1}{\L}$ on neighbouring levels.

\begin{lemma}[Refinement Relation, {\cite{Piel.2026}}]
    \label{lemma:hiermeshrel}
    Let $\l$ be an arbitrary level such that $\lzero \leq \l \leq \L$ and let $\Amat{k}{\l}{\l+1}$ be the subdivision matrix associated to $\Aop{k}{\l}{\l+1}$. Then, the basis functions $\constrQ{\SDFbasis}{k}{\l}{\L}{\coarseIdxone}$ and $\constrQ{\SDFbasis}{k}{\l+1}{\L}{\fineIdxone}$ satisfy the following \emph{refinement relation}
    \begin{equation}
        \label{eq:refinement_relation}
        \constrQ{\SDFbasis}{k}{\l}{\L}{\coarseIdxone} = \sum\limits_{\fineIdxone = 1}^{\sumdimlk{\l+1}{k}} \Amatij{k}{\l}{\l+1}{\fineIdxone}{\coarseIdxone} \; \constrQ{\SDFbasis}{k}{\l+1}{\L}{\fineIdxone} \; .
    \end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
    See~\cite{Piel.2026}.
\end{proof}

We will capture relationships between different basis functions across the mesh hierarchy through \textit{stencils}. Since there is a one-to-one correspondence between basis functions and simplices, we will formulate these stencils as maps between (sets of) simplices across the hierarchy. Thus, formally, a stencil is a map $\powerset(\K^k_{\l}) \to \powerset\!\bigl(\bigsqcup_{\l=\lzero}^{\L}\bigsqcup_{k=0}^{2}\K^k_\l\bigr)$, where $\powerset$ denotes the powerset.

We choose to formulate stencils with respect to simplices instead of basis functions because that enables us to use the adjacency operator $\KmKnl{m}{n}{\l}$ and refinement operators $\KKltoL{k}{\l}{\lone}$ introduced in~\cite{Piel.2026}. The remainder of this section sets up refinement stencils based on the refinement relation~\eqref{eq:refinement_relation}, while the next section lays the foundations for the derivative stencil by investigating the relation of basis functions of different form degrees.

The hierarchical refinement relation in Lemma~\ref{lemma:hiermeshrel} provides a way to re-express a basis function $\constrQ{\SDFbasis}{k}{\l}{\L}{\coarseIdxone}$ as a linear combination of finer basis functions. This idea of expressing coarse basis functions through finer basis functions is a core concept in this work. The following definition introduces the corresponding formalism called refinement stencils. After that, we highlight one particular known refinement stencil and then present a construction for more general refinement stencils.

\begin{definition}[Refinement Stencil]
    \label{def:deep_ref_stencil}
    Let $\constrQ{\SDFbasis}{k}{\l}{\L}{\coarseIdxone}$ denote the basis function of $\SDFspace{k}{\l}{\L}$ that is associated to the simplex $\simplex{k}{\l}{\coarseIdxone}$.
    A refinement stencil is a collection of simplices $\simplex{k}{\lone}{\fineIdxone}$ across the finer levels $\lone \in \{\l\!+\!1, \, \hdots, \L\}$ whose associated basis functions $\constrQ{\SDFbasis}{k}{\lone}{\L}{\fineIdxone}$ can exactly re-express $\constrQ{\SDFbasis}{k}{\l}{\L}{\coarseIdxone}$. More concretely, a refinement stencil $\RSt\big(\simplex{k}{l}{\coarseIdxone}\big)$ is a map
    \begin{equation}
        \label{equ:refstencilunion}
        \RSt : \K^k_{\l} \to \powerset \Big(\bigcup_{\lone = \l+1}^{\L} \K^k_{\lone} \Big), \quad  \simplex{k}{\l}{\coarseIdxone} \mapsto \RSt \big( \simplex{k}{\l}{\coarseIdxone} \big)
    \end{equation}
    such that there exist coefficients $\constrQ{c}{k}{\lone}{\l}{\fineIdxone} \neq 0$ satisfying
    \begin{equation}
        \constrQ{\SDFbasis}{k}{\l}{\L}{\coarseIdxone} = \sum_{\simplex{k}{\lone}{\fineIdxone} \in \RSt \big( \simplex{k}{\l}{\coarseIdxone} \big)} \constrQ{c}{k}{\robert{\lone}}{\robert{\l}}{\fineIdxone} \; \constrQ{\SDFbasis}{k}{\lone}{\L}{\fineIdxone}.
    \end{equation}
\end{definition}

The coefficients $\constrQ{c}{k}{\lone}{\l}{\fineIdxone}$ above are connected to the non-zero entries of the subdivision matrices. In fact, we can compute them by tracing back the contributions of certain fine basis functions to the coarse one analogously to the refinement relation~(\eqref{eq:refinement_relation}). The only difference is that the refinement stencil $\RSt \big( \simplex{k}{\l}{\coarseIdxone} \big)$ now contains simplices across different refinement levels, i.e. $\lone$ is not constant for $\simplex{k}{\lone}{\fineIdxone}$. We are only concerned with the existence of the coefficients $\constrQ{c}{k}{\lone}{\l}{\fineIdxone}$ because our goal is typically not to exactly re-express the coarse basis functions but to test wether they are in the space spanned by some finer basis functions. That is why we are going to formulate indicators for such linear dependence of coarse basis functions based on the existence of refinement stencils.

In the following, we will systematically construct refinement stencils by iterating the canonical refinement stencil defined next. According to Equation~\eqref{eq:refinement_relation}, all basis functions $\constrQ{\SDFbasis}{k}{\l+1}{\L}{\fineIdxone}$ with non-zero entries $\Amatij{k}{\l}{\l+1}{\fineIdxone}{\coarseIdxone} \neq 0$ are required to re-express the coarse basis function $\constrQ{\SDFbasis}{k}{\l}{\L}{\coarseIdxone}$. The following definition formalises this selection of basis functions.

\begin{definition}[Canonical Refinement Stencil]
    Let $\constrQ{\SDFbasis}{k}{\l}{\L}{\coarseIdxone}$ denote the basis function of $\SDFspace{k}{\l}{\L}$ that is associated to the simplex $\simplex{k}{\l}{\coarseIdxone}$. The canonical refinement $\cRSt\big( \simplex{k}{\l}{\coarseIdxone}\big)$ of $\constrQ{\SDFbasis}{k}{\l}{\L}{\coarseIdxone}$ is the map
    \begin{eqnarray}
        \cRSt: \K^k_{\l} \to \powerset\big( \K^k_{\l+1}\big), \qquad \simplex{k}{\l}{\coarseIdxone} \mapsto \Big\{ \simplex{k}{\l+1}{\fineIdxone} \in \K^k_{\l+1} \colon \; \Amatij{k}{\l}{\l+1}{\fineIdxone}{\coarseIdxone} \neq 0 \Big\}.
    \end{eqnarray}
\end{definition}

We call this refinement stencil canonical because it is the only one that contains simplices solely on level $\l\!+\!1$ and is thus uniquely determined by the refinement relation~\eqref{eq:refinement_relation}. Figure~\ref{fig:refinement_stencils} visualises the canonical refinement stencils of $k$-form DoFs and shows that we can obtain the canonical refinement stencil by going to the adjacent faces of $\simplex{k}{\l}{\coarseIdxone}$, refining them, taking their finer vertices and finally all fine $k$-simplices adjacent to these vertices. \cite{companion_paper} shows that we can construct all meaningful refinement stencils $\RSt$ across the entire mesh hierarchy by recursively applying the canonical refinement stencil $\cRSt$.

To guarantee linear independence of the selected active basis functions, we need to ensure that every refinement stencil of every active DoF contains at least one inactive DoF. In this paper, the refinement stencil serves as visual indicator of linear independence and is a key motivation to define the admissible domain sequence in Section~\ref{subsec:selecting_active_dofs}.

\subsection{Derivatives of basis functions}
\label{subsec:derivatives_of_basis_functions}

We now set up the differential structure of the subdivision $k$-form spaces. First,~\cite{Piel.2026} shows that the uniform subdivision $k$-form spaces comprise the de Rham complex
\begin{equation}
\begin{tikzcd}
  \constrVec{\mathfrak{R}}{}{\l}{\L}\colon \quad 0 \arrow[r] & \SDFspace{0}{\l}{\L} \arrow[r, "\extd"] & \SDFspace{1}{\l}{\L} \arrow[r, "\extd"] & \SDFspace{2}{\l}{\L} \arrow[r] & 0.
\end{tikzcd}
\end{equation}
on all intermediate levels $\l$ with $\lzero \leq \l \leq \l \leq \L$. Throughout this work, we do not distinguish between $\extd : \FEECspace{k}{\l} \to \FEECspace{k+1}{\l}$ and $\auxextd : \SDFspace{k}{\l}{\L} \to \SDFspace{k+1}{\l}{\L}$ like in~\cite{Piel.2026}. We will denote both operators by $\extd$ because they largely coincide for our purposes.

The coordinate expression of the action of the exterior derivative $\extd$ on the spaces $\SDFspace{k}{\l}{\L}$ follows directly from the coarse-level oriented incidence matrix $\Dmat{k}{\l}$~\citep{Hirani.2003,Desbrun.2006} by
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:derivative_relation_basis}
    \extd \constrQ{\SDFbasis}{k}{\l}{\L}{\coarseIdxone} = \sum_{\coarseIdxtwo=1}^{\sumdimlk{\l}{k+1}} \Dmatij{k}{\l}{\coarseIdxtwo}{\coarseIdxone} \, \constrQ{\SDFbasis}{k+1}{\l}{\L}{\coarseIdxtwo}.
\end{equation}

Since $\Dmat{k}{\l}$ is the signed incidence matrix of $\T_\l$, only the $(k\!+\!1)$-simplices of $\T_\l$ adjacent to $\simplex{k}{\l}{\coarseIdxone}$ appear with non-zero coefficients. In the context of splines, this is analogous to forward-differencing, in particular for triangular splines like box splines~\citep{de_boor_box_1993}.

\begin{definition}[Derivative Stencil]
    \label{def:derivative_stencil}
    Let $\constrQ{\SDFbasis}{k}{\l}{\L}{\coarseIdxone}$ be a basis function of $\SDFspace{k}{\l}{\L}$ associated with the $k$-simplex $\simplex{k}{\l}{\coarseIdxone} \in \K^k_{\l}$. The derivative stencil $\DSt$ is a map 
    \begin{equation}
        \DSt : \K^k_{\l} \to \powerset \big( \K^{k+1}_{\l} \big), \qquad \simplex{k}{\l}{\coarseIdxone} \; \mapsto \; \big\{ \simplex{k+1}{\l}{\coarseIdxtwo}: \; \Dmatij{k}{\l}{\coarseIdxtwo}{\coarseIdxone} \neq 0 \big\}.
    \end{equation}
\end{definition}

The derivative stencil is the key concept to ensure that the derivative of every active $k$-form basis function can be expressed exactly in the $(k\!+\!1)$-form space and that the resulting spaces constitute a differential complex. For the Wang schemes~\citep{Wang.2006,Wang.2008}, Definition~\ref{def:derivative_stencil} implies that the derivative stencil of a $k$-form simplex $\simplex{k}{\l}{\coarseIdxone}$ is given by $\DSt\big(\simplex{k}{\l}{\coarseIdxone}\big) = \K^{k+1}\K^k_{\l}\big(\simplex{k}{\l}{\coarseIdxone}\big)$, see~\cite{Piel.2026}. Figure~\ref{fig:derivative_stencils} visualises these refinement stencils.

To obtain a differential complex, we need to ensure that the derivative stencil of every active $k$-form basis function can be represented by active $k\!+\!1$-form basis functions of the next space in the complex, either because it is contained direcly or because it has an active refinement stencil. This property is a key constraint for the choice of active DoFs in Subsection~\ref{subsec:selecting_active_dofs}.

\subsection{Selection of active degrees of freedom}
\label{subsec:selecting_active_dofs}

Section~\ref{subsec:computational_representations} established that constructing the hierarchical subdivision matrix $\Amat{k}{\activeDoFs}{\L}$ reduces to choosing, at each level $\l$, an index set $\activeDoFSet{k}{\l} \subset \K^k_\l$ of active simplices. Each such choice selects the corresponding columns of $\Amat{k}{\l}{\L}$, assembling $\Amat{k}{\activeDoFs}{\L}$ block by block as in~\eqref{eq:hierarchical_matrix_blocks}. This section addresses the question left open there: which choices of $\activeDoFSet{k}{\l}$ are admissible? Recall that collecting the active simplices across levels yields the active DoFs
\begin{equation}
    \label{def:activeDoFs}
    \activeDoFSet{k}{} \coloneq \bigcup_{\l=\lzero}^{\L} \;\, \activeDoFSet{k}{\l}.
\end{equation}

We now provide a generic definition of the adaptive subdivision $k$-form spaces induced by an arbitrary set of active DoFs $\activeDoFSet{k}{}$.

\begin{definition}[Adaptive subdivision differential form spaces]
    \label{def:generic_adaptive_function_spaces}
    Fix an initial mesh $\T_{\lzero}$ with finest level $\L$ and select the active DoFs $\activeDoFSet{k}{}$. The \emph{active basis functions} of the \emph{adaptive subdivision $k$-form spaces} $\activeSDFspaces{k}{}$ are given by
    \begin{equation}
        \constrVec{\SDFbases}{k}{\activeDoFs}{\L} \coloneqq \bigcup_{\simplex{k}{\l}{\coarseIdxone} \in \activeDoFSet{k}{}} \big\{ \constrQ{\SDFbasis}{k}{\l}{\L}{\coarseIdxone} \big\} \qquad \text{and thus} \qquad \activeSDFspaces{k}{} \coloneqq \mathrm{span}\big( \constrVec{\SDFbases}{k}{\activeDoFs}{\L} \big).
    \end{equation}
\end{definition}

Not every choice of active DoFs $\activeDoFSet{k}{}$ yields a well-behaved hierarchical spaces $\activeSDFspaces{k}{}$, cf. Section~\ref{subsec:unrefinement_and_solve}. Most importantly, the active DoFs $\activeDoFSet{k}{}$ must together represent forms that cover the entire domain, must be linearly independent, and should constitute a exact complex. The remainder of this section introduces the tools needed to parameterise the active DoFs $\activeDoFSet{k}{}$ and to reason about these properties of the adaptive subdivision $k$-form spaces $\activeSDFspaces{k}{}$.

The requirements of linear indepence and expressing exterior differentials have a visual interpretation in terms of the refinement and derivative stencils introduced in the previous Sections~\ref{subsec:refinement_of_basis_functions} and~\ref{subsec:derivatives_of_basis_functions}. A selection of active basis functions is linearly independent when the active DoFs do not contain a refinement stencil for any individual active DoFs. Similarly, the spaces constitute a differential complex if the simplices in derivative stencil of any active basis function are themselves present in the active DoFs, either directly or through a refinement stencil.

The organising unit is a \emph{region} of a single level, and the object that
carries a whole adaptive space is a nested sequence of them, one per level. The following
definitions are taken from~\cite{companion_paper}, whose results are stated on them.

\begin{definition}[Region]
    \label{def:region}
    A \emph{region} $\R_\l \subseteq \F_\l(\T_\l)$ is a collection of faces of $\T_\l$. We write $\R_{\l \to \lone}$ for the faces obtained by repeatedly splitting $\R_\l$ from level $\l$ to $\lone$. The set complement $\R^\complement_\l \coloneq \F_\l(\T_\l) \setminus \R_\l$ of $\R_\l$ is also a region.
\end{definition}

\tobecut{
\begin{definition}[Patches of a region]
    \label{def:patch}
    A \emph{patch} $\calP_\l \subseteq \R_\l$ is a non-empty, edge-connected component of the region $\R_\l$. As a result, two patches of a region may share only vertices. We denote the number of patches of a region by $\patchNum(\R_\l)$ and enumerate the patches by $\R^n_\l$ with $n \in \{1, \, \dots, \, \patchNum(\R_\l)\}$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Boundary of a region]
    \label{def:region_boundary}
    The boundary $\boundary \R_\l \subseteq \E_\l(\T_\l)$ of a region $\R_\l$ is the set of edges of $\T_\l$ that are adjacent to exactly one face of $\R_\l$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Interface and domain boundary]
    \label{def:interface_and_domain_boundary}
    We decompose the boundary $\partial\R_\l$ of a region $\R_\l$ into a domain boundary part $\partial_\T \R_\l$ and an interface part $\partial_\I \R_\l$ given by
    \begin{equation}
        \label{eq:interface_convention}
        \partial_\T \R_\l \coloneq \partial \R_\l \cap \partial \T_\l \qquad \text{and} \qquad \partial_\I \R_\l \coloneq \partial \R_\l \setminus \partial_\T \R_\l.
    \end{equation}
    Further, we denote the number of vertex-connected components of the domain boundary $\partial_\T \R_\l$ by $\numComponents(\partial_\T \R_\l)$. Note that the operations in this definition are to be understood as operations on edge sets only.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Patch extension]
    \label{def:patch_extension}
    Let $\R_\l \subseteq \T_\l$ be a region. Patch extension $\extendedPatch{\cdot}$ adds one layer of faces along the interface of the region, i.e.
    \begin{equation}
        \R_\l \quad \mapsto \quad \extendedPatch{\R}_\l \coloneq \bigcup\limits_{\vertex{\l}{\coarseIdxone} \in \V_\l(\R_\l)} \FEl{\l} \big(\vertex{\l}{\coarseIdxone} \big) = \R_\l \cup \bigcup\limits_{\vertex{\l}{\coarseIdxone} \in \V_\l(\interface \R_\l)} \FEl{\l} \big(\vertex{\l}{\coarseIdxone} \big).
    \end{equation}
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Ancestor patch]
    \label{def:ancestor_patch}
    Let $\R_{\l \to \l+1} = \Aop{}{\l}{\l+1}(\R_\l)$ denote the refinement of the region $\R_\l \subseteq \T_\l$ by one level. Then, $\R_\l$ is ancestor of $\R_{\l \to \l+1}$, denoted as
    \begin{equation}
        \R_{\l \to \l+1} = \Aop{}{\l}{\l+1}(\R_\l) \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad \ancestorPatch{\R_{\l \to \l+1}} = \R_{\l}.
    \end{equation}
    The operator is defined precisely on the regions that are \emph{quadrisection-closed}
    at level $\l+1$ --- those of the form $\Aop{}{\l}{\l+1}(\R_\l)$, equivalently those containing
    all four children of every coarse face they meet --- and on no others. Writing
    $\ancestorPatch{\R}$ therefore carries the standing requirement that $\R$ be
    quadrisection-closed.
\end{definition}
}

\begin{definition}[Domain sequence]
    \label{def:domseq}
    A domain sequence $\domseq{\L} \coloneq \bigcup_{\l=\lzero}^{\L} \M_\l$ is a set of regions $\M_\l$ that satisfy
    \begin{equation}
        \M_\lzero = \T_\lzero \qquad \text{and} \qquad \ancestorPatch{\M_{\l+1}} \subseteq \M_\l.
    \end{equation}
    for $\l \in \{ \lzero, \, \dots, \, \L \}$. By convention, $\M_{\L+1} = \varnothing$. Typically, $\M_\l = \bigcup_{n=1}^{\mathrm{N_{\l}}} \calP^n_{\l}$ contains multiple disjoint patches $\calP^n_{\l}$. The set of all domain sequences is denoted by $\alldomseqs_\L$.
\end{definition}

The nesting condition carries more than it appears to. By Definition~\ref{def:ancestor_patch}
the left-hand side is defined only when $\M_{\l+1}$ is quadrisection-closed, so requiring it
to lie in $\M_\l$ asks two things at once: that each domain be a union of \emph{complete}
quadrisections, and that the coarse faces it refines belong to the previous domain. No region
of the sequence can therefore hold three of a face's four children.

\guidance{The level subsets used throughout carry over unchanged, now read off the regions: we write
$\M_{>\lone} = \bigcup_{\l = \lone+1}^{\L} \M_\l$, and likewise $\M_{\geq\lone}$,
$\M_{<\lone}$ and $\M_{\leq\lone}$. The faces a domain sequence resolves at exactly level
$\l$ --- the analogue of the old $\C_\l$ --- are $\M_\l \setminus \ancestorPatch{\M_{\l+1}}$,
the level-$\l$ region minus what it refines further.}

We refer to the set \robert{$\K^k(\C) \coloneq \bigcup_{\l=\lzero}^{\L} \K^k(\C_\l)$} as the \emph{patch DoFs}.
Thus, spanning an adaptive space using the patch DoFs of domain sequence $\C$ ensures that the entire domain is covered with active basis functions. It does not yet guarantee either linear independence or a differential complex. The following examples develops an intuition for the selection of additional active degrees of freedom and constraints regarding the domain sequence.

    \label{ex:selection}
    Consider the domain sequence shown at the top of Figure~\ref{fig:active_dofs}, in which
    $\M_0 = \T_0$ is the whole coarse mesh, $\M_1$ is its upper half, and $\M_2$ consists of
    the two left-most spoke regions of that half. Since $\M_2$ lies strictly inside $\M_1$,
    the sequence carries a level-$0$ to level-$2$ interface across the horizontal axis, and
    $\L \geq 2$.

    Read the selection one level at a time. At the finest level $\l = 2$ nothing is refined
    further, so $\ancestorPatch{\M_3} = \varnothing$ and nothing is removed: the active DoFs
    are exactly those interior to $\extendedPatch{\M}_2$. That extended region is $\M_2$
    together with one collar of level-$2$ faces along its interface, which is why row
    $\l = 2$ of Figure~\ref{fig:active_dofs} shows active DoFs reaching one face beyond the
    two refined spokes. The vertices, edges and faces sitting on the outer boundary of the
    collar are not interior to it and are stripped by
    Lemma~\ref{lemma:open_dofs_membership} --- a simplex is kept only when \emph{every}
    level-$2$ face containing it lies in the extended region.

    At $\l = 1$ both terms are non-empty. The insertion is again everything interior to
    $\extendedPatch{\M}_1$, one collar out from the upper half. The removal is everything
    interior to $\ancestorPatch{\M_2}$, the level-$1$ faces that $\M_2$ refines. This is what
    row $\l = 1$ shows: level-$1$ DoFs appear along the interface with the coarse lower half
    and around the two spokes, but the DoFs strictly inside the refined spokes are gone,
    because they are carried at level $2$ instead. Note that the removal strips only the
    \emph{interior} of $\ancestorPatch{\M_2}$; the DoFs on its interface survive, and they
    are exactly the extra level-$1$ DoFs visible between the level-$0$ and level-$2$ regions.

    At $\l = 0$ the region is the whole mesh, so $\extendedPatch{\M}_0 = \T_0$ has no
    interface to strip and the insertion is all of $\K^k_0$. The removal is the interior of
    $\ancestorPatch{\M_1}$, the upper half. Row $\l = 0$ therefore keeps the lower half and
    the DoFs along the horizontal axis, and drops everything strictly above it.

    The pattern is the general one: each level contributes the DoFs of the faces it resolves,
    plus the collar DoFs that the removal at that level does not reach.
\end{example}}

The selection described in Example~\ref{ex:selection} is stated below on the
regions of a domain sequence rather than patch by patch. Two auxiliary notions are needed,
both taken from~\cite{companion_paper}: the DoFs a region carries, and the ones that are
\emph{interior} to it in the sense that nothing outside the region touches them.

\begin{definition}[Region DoFs]
    \label{def:region_dofs}
    The DoFs $\patchDoFs{\R}{k}_\l$ of the region $\R_\l \subseteq \F_\l(\T_\l)$ are given by $\patchDoFs{\R}{k}_\l \coloneq \K^k_\l(\R_\l)$.
\end{definition}

\tobecut{
\begin{definition}[Edge set DoFs]
    \label{def:edge_set_dofs}
    The DoFs $\edgeDoFs{\Gamma}{k}_\l$ of an edge set $\Gamma_\l \in \E_\l(\T_\l)$ are given by
    \begin{equation}
        \edgeDoFs{\Gamma}{0}_\l \coloneq \bigcup\limits_{\edge{\l}{\coarseIdxone} \in \Gamma_\l} \VEl{\l}(\edge{\l}{\coarseIdxone}), \qquad \edgeDoFs{\Gamma}{1}_\l \coloneq \Gamma_\l, \qquad \edgeDoFs{\Gamma}{2}_\l = \varnothing
    \end{equation}
    for $k \in \{ 0, 1, 2\}$.
\end{definition}
}

\begin{definition}[Open region DoFs]
    \label{def:open_patch_dofs}
    Let $\R_\l \subseteq \T_\l$ be a region. The open patch DoFs $\openPatchDoFs{\cdot}{k}$ are the region DoFs of $\R_\l$ without the interface DoFs, i.e.
    \begin{equation}
        \openPatchDoFs{\R}{k}_\l \coloneq \patchDoFs{\R}{k}_\l \setminus \interfaceDoFs{\R_\l}{k}.
    \end{equation}
\end{definition}

\begin{lemma}[Open DoFs are those with all their faces inside]
    \label{lemma:open_dofs_membership}
    For a region $\R_\l \subseteq \T_\l$ and a simplex $\simplex{k}{\l}{\coarseIdxone} \in \patchDoFs{\R}{k}_\l$,
    \begin{equation}
        \label{eq:open_dofs_membership}
        \simplex{k}{\l}{\coarseIdxone} \in \openPatchDoFs{\R}{k}_\l
        \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad
        \F\K^k_\l\big(\simplex{k}{\l}{\coarseIdxone}\big) \subseteq \R_\l ,
    \end{equation}
    where $\F\K^k_\l(\simplex{k}{\l}{\coarseIdxone})$ are the level-$\l$ faces containing
    $\simplex{k}{\l}{\coarseIdxone}$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
    See~\cite{companion_paper}.
\end{proof}

Lemma~\ref{lemma:open_dofs_membership} is what makes the definition below checkable: openness
is a purely local test on the faces containing a simplex, with no reference to interfaces or
to neighbouring patches.

\begin{definition}[Active $k$-form degrees of freedom]
    \label{def:active_k_form_dofs}
    Let $\domseq{\L} = \bigcup_{\l = \lzero}^{\L} \M_\l$ be a domain sequence of $\T_\L$. The
    active $k$-form degrees of freedom $\activeDoFSet{k}{}(\domseq{\L})$ are defined level by
    level as
    \begin{equation}
        \label{eq:active_k_form_dofs}
        \activeDoFSet{k}{} \big(\domseq{\L}\big) \coloneqq \bigsqcup\limits_{\l = \lzero}^{\L}
            \Big( \underbrace{\openPatchDoFs{\extendedPatch{\M}}{k}_\l}_{\text{inserted}}
            \;\setminus\;
            \underbrace{\openPatchDoFs{\ancestorPatch{\M_{\l+1}}}{k}_\l}_{\text{removed}} \Big).
    \end{equation}
\end{definition}

\guidance{At each level the definition \emph{inserts} the DoFs interior to the extended region
$\extendedPatch{\M}_\l$ --- the level-$\l$ region together with one collar layer of faces
along its interface, Definition~\ref{def:patch_extension} --- and \emph{removes} those
interior to $\ancestorPatch{\M_{\l+1}}$, the part of the region that is refined further and
whose DoFs are therefore represented on finer levels instead. What survives is the DoFs of
the faces the sequence resolves at exactly level $\l$, together with the collar DoFs that
the removal does not strip. The union is disjoint because every DoF carries the level it is
taken from.}

\begin{definition}[Adaptive subdivision $k$-form spaces induced by a domain sequence]
    \label{def:adaptive_function_spaces}
    The adaptive subdivision $k$-form spaces $\hierSDFspace{k}{\C}{\L}$ and $\hierSDFspaceZero{k}{\C}{\L}$ (with vanishing trace) induced by the domain sequence $\C(\T_\L)$ are given by
    \begin{equation}
        \hierSDFspace{k}{\C}{\L} \coloneqq \mathrm{span}\big( \constrVec{\SDFbases}{k}{\activeDoFs(\C)}{\L} \big) \qquad \text{and} \qquad \hierSDFspaceZero{k}{\C}{\L} \coloneqq \mathrm{span}\big( \constrVec{\mathring{\SDFbases}}{k}{\activeDoFs(\C)}{\L} \big),
    \end{equation}
    where the active basis functions $\constrVec{\SDFbases}{k}{\activeDoFs(\C)}{\L}$ and $\constrVec{\mathring{\SDFbases}}{k}{\activeDoFs(\C)}{\L} \coloneq \constrVec{\SDFbases}{k}{(\activeDoFs(\C) \setminus \robert{\K^k(\partial \C)})}{\L}$ are constructed as in Definition~\ref{def:generic_adaptive_function_spaces}.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}
    \label{theorem:differential_complex}
    Let $\C$ be a domain sequence of the mesh $\T_\L$. The adaptive subdivision $k$-form spaces $\hierSDFspace{k}{\C}{\L}$ constitute the differential complex
    \begin{equation}
    \begin{tikzcd}
        \mathfrak{R}_\C: \qquad 0 \arrow[r] & \mathbb{R} \arrow[r, "\subset"] & \hierSDFspace{0}{\C}{\L} \arrow[r, "\extd"] & \hierSDFspace{1}{\C}{\L} \arrow[r, "\extd"] & \hierSDFspace{2}{\C}{\L} \arrow[r] & 0.
    \end{tikzcd}
    \end{equation}
    The adaptive subdivision $k$-form spaces with vanishing traces form the differential complex
    \begin{equation}
    \begin{tikzcd}
        \mathring{\mathfrak{R}}_\C: \qquad 0 \arrow[r] & \hierSDFspaceZero{0}{\C}{\L} \arrow[r, "\extd"] & \hierSDFspaceZero{1}{\C}{\L} \arrow[r, "\extd"] & \hierSDFspaceZero{2}{\C}{\L} \arrow[r, "\int"] & \mathbb{R} \arrow[r] & 0.
    \end{tikzcd}
    \end{equation}
\end{theorem}

The other essential properties of the adaptive $k$-form subdivision spaces, namely linear independence and correct cohomology of the discrete complex, are tied to the geometry of the regions that constitute the domain sequence, see~\cite{companion_paper}. This will be the subject of the following Section~\ref{subsec:subdiv_partition}.

\subsection{Operations on domain sequences}
\label{subsec:operations_on_coverings}

This section develops the necessary concepts to describe the two particular configurations that compromise linear independence and the cohomology of the complex. These foundations enable us to introduce the geometric predicates of pinch vertices and inadmissible faces in Section~\ref{subsec:subdiv_partition} which allow us to constrain the geometry of domain sequences and indicate the need for padding in the adaptive loop in Section~\ref{subsec:adaptive_loop}.

\smallskip

\robert{Note that the set of covering-interior vertices of $\C$ does not only contain the interior vertices of the patches of $\C$ but can also contain interface vertices and vertices on the boundary $\partial \C$. For example, Figure~\ref{fig:covering_interior_vertices} displays a domain sequence with nested refinement up to level $3$ towards its center. The vertices are colored by the level at which they become covering-interior. The outermost layer is covering-interior from level $0$ because its covering star contains only faces on level $0$. The next layer is covering-interior from level $1$ because the covering stars of its vertex classes contains only faces on level $0$ and $1$. This logic applies to all layers in the figure until we reach the finest level $3$, at which all the remaining vertex classes become covering-interior.}

\smallskip

The remainder of this section introduces operators that enable the kinds of refinement that we will later need for the adaptive loop in Section~\ref{subsec:adaptive_loop}. The \emph{face quadrisection operator} $\quadriF(\C, \simplexsubset{2}{\l})$ refines a \emph{single-level} set of faces $\simplexsubset{2}{\l} \subseteq \F(\C_\l)$ to level $\l+1$ by replacing each face with its four children, that is, it removes the faces $\simplexsubset{2}{\l}$ from level $\l$ and adds their children on level $\l+1$, leaving all other levels untouched.

\guidance{
\begin{definition}[Face quadrisection operator]
    \label{def:face_quadrisection_operator}
    Let $\domseq{\L} \in \alldomseqs$ be a domain sequence with regions $\M_\l$,
    $\l \in \{ \lzero, \, \dots \,, \L\}$, and let $\simplexsubset{2}{\l} \subseteq \F_\l(\M_\l)$
    be a set of faces marked for refinement. The \emph{face quadrisection operator}
    \begin{equation}
        \quadriF \; \colon \; \alldomseqs \times \powerset\big( \F_\l \big) \; \longrightarrow \; \alldomseqs
    \end{equation}
    is defined by
    \begin{equation}
        \label{eq:def_face_quadrisection_operator}
        \quadriF \big( \domseq{\L}, \, \simplexsubset{2}{\l} \big) \coloneq \M_{\leq\l} \; \cup \; \big( \M_{\l+1} \cup \Aop{}{\l}{\l+1}(\simplexsubset{2}{\l}) \big) \; \cup \; \M_{\geq \l+2}.
    \end{equation}
\end{definition}

Note that if $\simplexsubset{2}{\l} \subseteq \ancestorPatch{\M_{\l+1}}$, then
$\quadriF ( \domseq{\L}, \simplexsubset{2}{\l} ) = \domseq{\L}$ is the identity because all
marked faces on level $\l$ already are in the domain sequence's region on level $\l+1$.

Restricting the second argument to the inadmissible faces sharpens the image, which is what
the \textsc{Pad} step of Section~\ref{subsec:padding} relies on.

\begin{lemma}[Admissibility padding preserves refinement exteriority]
    \label{lemma:quadriF_preserves_admissibility}
    Let $\domseq{\L} \in \alldomseqs$ and let
    $\F^{\mathrm{inad}}_\l \subseteq \F_\l(\M_\l)$ be the set of \emph{all} inadmissible faces on
    level $\l$. Then a vertex $\vertex{\l}{\coarseIdxone} \in \V_\l(\T_\l)$ is refinement-exterior
    in $\quadriF ( \domseq{\L}, \F^{\mathrm{inad}}_\l )$ if and only if it is refinement-exterior in
    $\domseq{\L}$. Consequently every face admissible in $\domseq{\L}$ remains admissible, and
    \begin{equation}
        \quadriF \big( \cdot, \, \F^{\mathrm{inad}}_\l \big) \; \colon \; \alldomseqs \; \longrightarrow \;
        \big\{ \domseq{\L} \in \alldomseqs \; : \; \text{clause (i) of Definition~\ref{def:subdiv_partition} holds on level } \l \big\}.
    \end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
    Refinement fans only grow under $\quadriF$, so a vertex that is not refinement-exterior in
    $\domseq{\L}$ is not refinement-exterior afterwards; only the converse direction has content.
    Let $\vertex{\l}{\coarseIdxone}$ be refinement-exterior in $\domseq{\L}$ and suppose some
    $\face{\l}{\coarseIdxtwo} \in \F^{\mathrm{inad}}_\l$ were incident to it. By
    Definition~\ref{def:admissible_face} an inadmissible face has \emph{no} refinement-exterior
    vertex, contradicting the assumption on $\vertex{\l}{\coarseIdxone}$. Hence no face of
    $\F^{\mathrm{inad}}_\l$ touches a refinement-exterior vertex, so
    $\refFan{\l}(\vertex{\l}{\coarseIdxone})$ is unchanged and stays empty.

    Every admissible face therefore keeps its refinement-exterior witness. The refined faces
    themselves leave level $\l$; their children are admissible on level $\l+1$, since the middle
    child's three vertices are the parent's edge midpoints, which are created at level $\l+1$ and
    are refinement-exterior there whenever $\M_{\l+2}$ is unchanged by this step.
\end{proof}

The lemma does \emph{not} extend to the full admissibility of
Definition~\ref{def:subdiv_partition}. Enlarging a refinement fan can raise the number of its
maximal runs --- an empty fan $[\,u\,u\,u\,u\,u\,u\,]$ refined in positions $1$ and $4$ becomes
$[\,r\,u\,u\,r\,u\,u\,]$, two refinement arcs --- so admissibility padding can create pinch
vertices, and clause~(ii) may fail on the image. Together with
Remark~\ref{rem:padding_order}, which records the opposite failure, the two repairs violate one
another's invariants in both directions. This is exactly why \textsc{Pad} iterates rather than
terminating after one sweep, and why $\allpartitions$ is characterised as the set of fixed points
of that iteration.}

\robert{In contrast to the that, the \emph{vertex quadrisection operator} $\quadriV{\targetlevel}(\C, \coveringStar(\vertclass{\coarseIdxtwo}))$ refines a whole covering star by nested face quadrisections from the source level $\sourcelevel_\coarseIdxtwo$ to the target level $\targetlevel_\coarseIdxtwo$.}

\begin{definition}[Vertex quadrisection operator]
    \label{def:vertex_quadrisection_operator_birth_level}
    Let $\domseq{\L} \in \alldomseqs$ and let $\vertex{\l}{\coarseIdxtwo}$ be a vertex with birth level $\birthLevel_\coarseIdxtwo$. Choose a target level $\targetlevel$ with
    $\birthLevel_\coarseIdxtwo \leq \targetlevel \leq \L$. The \emph{vertex quadrisection operator}
    \begin{equation}
        \quadriV{\targetlevel} \; \colon \; \alldomseqs \times \V \; \longrightarrow \; \alldomseqs
    \end{equation}
    is constructed by iteratively vertex stars:
    \begin{enumerate}[topsep=3pt, itemsep=-2pt]%[nolistsep]
        \item initialise $\C^{\birthLevel_\coarseIdxtwo} = \domseq{\L}$,
        \item for $\l = \birthLevel_\coarseIdxtwo, \, \dots, \, \targetlevel - 1$, apply the face quadrisection operator to the level-$\l$ part of the star of $\vertex{\l}{\coarseIdxtwo}$ \emph{in the current sequence} $\C^\l$, i.e.
        \begin{equation}
            \label{eq:vertex_quadrisection}
            \C^{\l+1} = \quadriF \big( \C^\l, \; \FVl{\l}(\vertex{\l}{\coarseIdxtwo}) \cap \M_\l \big),
        \end{equation}
        \vspace{-7mm}
        \item set $\quadriV{\targetlevel} \big( \domseq{\L}, \vertex{\l}{\coarseIdxtwo} \big) \coloneq \C^{\targetlevel}$.
    \end{enumerate}
    The target level rides on the operator rather than in an argument slot, since it selects
    \emph{which} map is applied rather than what it is applied to.
\end{definition}

Recomputing the covering star of $\vertclass{\coarseIdxtwo}$ of $\C^\l$ at each step is what drags the coarse faces up through \emph{every} intermediate level up to $\targetlevel$. The two operators are distinguished by their superscript: $\quadriF$ refines a set of faces flatly from level $\l$ to $\l\!+\!1$, while $\quadriV{\targetlevel}$ refines a star gradedly toward its vertex class, and thus only the graded vertex quadrisection operator carries a target level. Figure~\ref{fig:covering_star_refined} displays the result of applying $\quadriV{\targetlevel\coarseIdxtwo + 1}(\C, \vertclass{\coarseIdxtwo})$ to the covering star from Figure~\ref{fig:covering_star}. It first homogenises the neighbourhood of $\vertclass{\coarseIdxtwo}$ to its target level $\targetlevel_\coarseIdxtwo$ and then applies one more level of refinement to the now homogeneous active faces on level $\targetlevel_\coarseIdxtwo$ adjacent to $\vertclass{\coarseIdxtwo}$.

The quadrisection operators enable consistent refinement of the adaptive subdivision $k$-form spaces $\hierSDFspace{k}{\C}{\L}$ in the sense that
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:quadrisection_monotone}
    \hierSDFspace{k}{\C}{\L} \subseteq \hierSDFspace{k}{\quadriF(\C, \simplexsubset{2}{\l})}{\L} \qquad \text{and} \qquad \hierSDFspace{k}{\C}{\L} \subseteq \hierSDFspace{k}{\quadriV{\targetlevel}(\C, \vertclass{\coarseIdxtwo})}{\L}.
\end{equation}
The quadrisection operators defined in this section are the basis upon which we will later built the algorithms in the adaptive loop in Section~\ref{subsec:adaptive_loop}.

\subsection{Admissible domain sequences and properties of the adaptive spaces}
\label{subsec:subdiv_partition}

We are now ready to define the first geometric predicate of face admissibility.

\guidance{Both predicates are stated on the \emph{refinement fan}, following~\cite{companion_paper}:
the faces at a vertex that the sequence refines beyond the current level. Phrasing them on the
refined side rather than the coarse one is what makes them level-local --- the removed region
\emph{is} the refined region seen at level $\l$, so the fan's components are the local components
of the removed region, and the coarse-side disconnection is a consequence rather than the cause.

\begin{definition}[Refinement fan]
    \label{def:refinement_fan}
    Let $\domseq{\L}$ be a domain sequence with regions $\M_\l$. The \emph{refinement fan} $\refFan{\l} \big( \vertex{\l}{\coarseIdxone} \big)$ of a vertex $\vertex{\l}{\coarseIdxone} \in \V_\l(\T_\l)$ contains all incident faces of $\vertex{\l}{\coarseIdxone}$ that are refined beyond level $\l$ in the domain sequence. The refinement fan is defined as
    \begin{equation}
        \refFan{\l} \big( \vertex{\l}{\coarseIdxone} \big) \coloneq \FVl{\l} \big( \vertex{\l}{\coarseIdxone} \big) \cap \ancestorPatch{\M_{\l+1}}.
    \end{equation}
\end{definition}

The refinement fan allows us to state the two important geometric predicates for admissibility.

\begin{definition}[Refinement-exterior vertex]
    \label{def:refinement_exterior_vertex}
    Let $\domseq{\L}$ be a domain sequence. A vertex $\vertex{\l}{\coarseIdxone} \in \V_\l(\T_\l)$ is \emph{refinement-exterior} at level $\l$ if its refinement fan in $\domseq{\L}$ is empty on that level, i.e. $\refFan{\l} \big( \vertex{\l}{\coarseIdxone} \big) = \varnothing$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Admissible face]
    \label{def:admissible_face}
    A face $\face{\l}{\coarseIdxone} \in \F(\domseq{\L})$ is \emph{admissible} if at least one of its vertices is
    refinement-exterior at level $\l$. Otherwise, it is \emph{inadmissible}.
\end{definition}}

Figure~\ref{fig:inadmissible_faces} shows a domain sequence whose faces on level $1$ are all admissible while the faces on level $0$ (purple) are inadmissible. As we will see in Section~\ref{subsec:exactness_atomic}, the adaptive subdivision $k$-form spaces on this domain sequence have some interesting properties: There are $3$ linearly dependent active vertex DoFs, $18$ linearly dependent active edge DoFs and $15$ linearly dependent active face DoFs. At the same time, the spaces on this domain sequence have the correct cohomology of $\mathrm{dim}(\mathcal{H}^0) = 1$ and $\mathrm{dim}(\mathcal{H}^1) = \mathrm{dim}(\mathcal{H}^2) = 0$.

\smallskip

The second predicate is that of a pinch vertex.

\tobecut{\begin{definition}[pinch vertex]
    % \label{def:pinch_vertex}
    Let $\vertclass{\coarseIdxtwo} \in \widehat{\V}_\C$ be a vertex equivalence class of $\C$ with target level $\targetlevel_\coarseIdxtwo$ and compute the number
    \begin{equation}
        \mathrm{N}_\coarseIdxtwo \coloneq \Big\vert \big\{ \edge{\targetlevel_\coarseIdxtwo}{\coarseIdxone} \in \E\V(\vertex{\targetlevel_\coarseIdxtwo}{\coarseIdxtwo}) \; : \; \F\E(\edge{\targetlevel_\coarseIdxtwo}{\coarseIdxone}) \cap \C_{\targetlevel_\coarseIdxtwo} \neq \varnothing \;\;\; \text{and} \;\;\; \F\E(\edge{\targetlevel_\coarseIdxtwo}{\coarseIdxone}) \setminus \C_{\targetlevel_\coarseIdxtwo} \neq \varnothing \big\} \Big\vert
    \end{equation}
    of edges whose adjacent faces split into an active and an inactive face on level $\targetlevel_\coarseIdxtwo$, see Figure~\ref{fig:pinches}. If $\edge{\targetlevel_\coarseIdxtwo}{\coarseIdxone}$ is a boundary edge in $\E(\partial\T_{\targetlevel_\coarseIdxtwo})$, we always count it as having at least one inactive adjacent face DoF to represent the exterior of $\T_{\targetlevel_\coarseIdxtwo}$. The number $\mathrm{N}_\coarseIdxtwo$ will then always be even. \emph{number of disconnected arcs} $\gamma_\coarseIdxtwo$ of the vertex class $\vertclass{\coarseIdxtwo}$ is given by
    \begin{equation}
        \gamma_\coarseIdxtwo = \begin{cases}
            1, \quad & \text{if} \;\; \mathrm{N}_\coarseIdxtwo = 0, \\
            \tfrac{1}{2} \mathrm{N}_\coarseIdxtwo, \quad & \text{otherwise}.
        \end{cases}
    \end{equation}
    If $\gamma_\coarseIdxtwo \geq 2$, the vertex class $\vertclass{\coarseIdxtwo}$ is a called \emph{pinch} vertex.
\end{definition}}

\guidance{The counting rule above is superseded on two counts, both settled
in~\cite{companion_paper}. It is evaluated at the target level $\targetlevel_\coarseIdxtwo$
alone, where the predicate has to hold at \emph{every} level; and its convention of treating
a domain-boundary edge as carrying a phantom inactive face outside $\T_\l$ closes the boundary
fan into a cycle, which misclassifies one configuration --- see the discussion after
Definition~\ref{def:subdiv_partition}. The replacement counts runs on the refinement fan
directly.

\begin{definition}[Refinement arcs of a vertex]
    \label{def:refinement_arcs}
    Let $\vertex{\l}{\coarseIdxone} \in \V(\domseq{\L})$. The \emph{refinement arcs of $\vertex{\l}{\coarseIdxone}$} are the maximal runs of consecutive faces in its cyclic (or, at a boundary vertex, linear) refinement fan $\refFan{\l}(\vertex{\l}{\coarseIdxone})$. Two faces of the same arc are connected through shared level-$\l$ edges at $\vertex{\l}{\coarseIdxone}$ while faces of distinct arcs are not.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Pinch vertex]
    \label{def:pinch_vertex}
    A vertex $\vertex{\l}{\coarseIdxone} \in \V(\domseq{\L})$ is a \emph{pinch vertex} if it has two or more refinement arcs.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Boundary pinch vertex]
    \label{def:boundary_pinch}
    Let $\vertex{\l}{\coarseIdxone} \in \V{\domseq{\L}} \cap \partial\T_\l$ lie on the boundary of $\T_\l$. Its refinement fan $\refFan{\l}(\vertex{\l}{\coarseIdxone})$ then contains two faces that contain domain boundary edges. We call a refinement arc \emph{anchored} if it contains such a face. The vertex $\vertex{\l}{\coarseIdxone}$ is a \emph{boundary pinch} it has at least one refinement arc that is not anchored.
\end{definition}}

The two predicates allow us to define \emph{admissible domain sequences} as a subset of domain sequences.

\begin{definition}[Admissible domain sequence]
    \label{def:subdiv_partition}
    A domain sequence $\domseq{\L}(\T_\L)$ is an \emph{admissible domain sequence} iff
    \begin{itemize}
        \item[(i)] every face of $\domseq{\L}$ is admissible,
        \item[(ii)] no vertex of $\domseq{\L}$ is a pinch vertex,
        \item[(iii)] no domain boundary vertex of $\domseq{\L}$ is a boundary pinch vertex.
    \end{itemize}
    We denote the set of admissible domain sequences by $\allpartitions(\T_\L)$.
\end{definition}

\guidance{Clause (ii) is deliberately \emph{not} restricted to interior vertices.}

Finally, we are going to re-state the two main theorems of \cite{companion_paper} regarding linear independence and the exactness of the complex.

\begin{theorem}
    \label{theorem:linear_independence}
    Let $\C \in \allpartitions$ be a admissible domain sequence of the mesh $\T_\L$. The active basis functions selected by Def.~\ref{def:active_k_form_dofs} of the adaptive spaces $\hierSDFspace{k}{\C}{\L}$ are linearly independent.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
    Linear dependencies only occur at inadmissible faces but a admissible domain sequence does not contain any inadmissible faces. See \cite{companion_paper} for the entire proof.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}
    \label{theorem:exactness}
    Let $\C \in \allpartitions$ be a admissible domain sequence. Then, the cohomology groups of the complexes $\mathfrak{R}_\C$ and $\mathring{\mathfrak{R}}_\C$ of adaptive $k$-form subdivision spaces from Theorem~\ref{theorem:differential_complex} are isomorphic to the ones of absolute and relative de Rham complexes, respectively.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
    The proof is built upon the arguments in~\cite{Evans.2020} around removing coarse spaces and inserting fine spaces instead, while keeping track of their cohomologies. See \cite{companion_paper} for the detailed proof in the subdivision context.
\end{proof}

Admissible domain sequences respect the geometric constraints necessary for linear independence and reproducing the correct cohomology. Unfortunately, in contrast to general domain sequences $\allcoverings$, admissible domain sequences are not closed under the quadrisection operator $\quadriF(\C, \cdot)$ and $\quadriV{\targetlevel}(\C, \cdot)$, meaning that they are maps $\allpartitions \to \allcoverings$. Thus, naive refinement of admissible domain sequences can reintroduce inadmissible geometry, for example a pinch vertex. This is exactly why Section~\ref{subsec:adaptive_loop} sets up a padding step that repairs these geometric defects and turns the domain sequence into a admissible domain sequence again.

\section{Implementation and adaptive loop}

This section turns the constructions of Section~\ref{sec:construction_of_hierarchical_subdiv_spaces} into a working adaptive algorithm. Section~\ref{subsec:implementation_active_dofs} shows how the active DoF selection and the adaptive subdivision matrices $\Amat{k}{\activeDoFs}{\L}$ are assembled in practice. Section~\ref{subsec:adaptive_loop} then embeds this machinery in a standard \textsc{Solve}--\textsc{Estimate}--\textsc{Mark}--\textsc{Refine}--\textsc{Pad} adaptive loop, where the additional \textsc{Pad} restores the domain sequence output by \textsc{Refine} to an admissible one (Definition~\ref{def:subdiv_partition}) after each refinement via pinch resolution, admissibility padding, and grading.

\subsection{Assembling the hierarchical subdivision matrix}
\label{subsec:implementation_active_dofs}

The implementation of the active subdivision spaces $\hierSDFspace{k}{\C}{\L}$ proceeds in two stages. 
Given a admissible domain sequence $\C$, we first identify the active degrees of
freedom $\D^k$ for each form degree $k \in \{0,1,2\}$ 
using Algorithm~\ref{alg:buildactivedofs} in the appendix. Algorithm~\ref{alg:buildsubdivmatrix} then assembles, for each form
degree $k$, the sparse matrix $\Amat{k}{\activeDoFs}{\L}$ whose columns are
the level-$\L$ coefficient expansions of the active basis functions $\Phi_{\D^k (\C)}$, see Equations~\eqref{eq:hierarchical_form_compact} and~\eqref{eq:hierarchical_matrix_blocks}. Galerkin projection of all
bilinear forms to the active space then reduces to standard sparse
matrix-matrix products, without any re-assembly of integrals, see for example Equation~\eqref{eq:FE_matrix_assembly}.

\guidance{Definition~\ref{def:active_k_form_dofs} is already stated level by level, and its $\l$-th term
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:active_k_form_dofs_per_level}
    \activeDoFSet{k}{\l} \big( \domseq{\L} \big) = \openPatchDoFs{\extendedPatch{\M}}{k}_\l
        \setminus \openPatchDoFs{\ancestorPatch{\M_{\l+1}}}{k}_\l
\end{equation}
is exactly what the implementation computes at level $\l$. The inserted set covers the
level-$\l$ region together with one layer of faces along its interface, and the removed set
strips what is carried on finer levels.

Algorithm~\ref{alg:buildactivedofs} in Appendix~\ref{app:active_dof_pseudocode} gives this in
full. It is indexed by level $\l = \lzero, \, \dots, \, \L$, mirroring
$\activeDoFSet{k}{}(\domseq{\L}) = \bigsqcup_\l \activeDoFSet{k}{\l}(\domseq{\L})$, and its
three primitives are the region $\M_\l$, the extension $\extendedPatch{\M}_\l$, and the
membership test of Lemma~\ref{lemma:open_dofs_membership}.}

Once the active DoFs $\D^k$ have been identified, the adaptive subdivision matrix
$\Amat{k}{\activeDoFs}{\L}$ is assembled column by column. According to Equation~\eqref{eq:hierarchical_matrix_blocks}, Algorithm~\ref{alg:buildsubdivmatrix} extracts the columns of $\Amat{k}{\l}{\L}$ corresponding to the active DoFs $\activeDoFSet{k}{\l}$ on level $\l$.

\robert{\begin{algorithm}[tb]
\caption{$\textsc{BuildSubdivMatrix}(\D, k)$: adaptive subdivision matrix}
\label{alg:buildsubdivmatrix}
\KwIn{active DoFs $\D$, form degree $k$, uniform subdivision matrices $\Amat{k}{\l}{\L}$}
\KwOut{adaptive subdivision matrix $\Amat{k}{\activeDoFs}{\L}$}
\BlankLine
$\Amat{k}{\activeDoFs}{\L} \leftarrow (\vert \K^k_\L \vert \times \vert \D^k \vert)$ sparse zero matrix\;
$\mathrm{col} \leftarrow 0$\;
\For{$\l = 0, \, \dots, \, \L$}{
    \For{each simplex $\simplex{k}{\l}{\coarseIdxone} \in \D^k_\l$}{
        $\Amat{k}{\activeDoFs}{\L}[\,:\,,\mathrm{col}] \leftarrow \Amat{k}{\l}{\L}[\,:\,, \coarseIdxone]$\;
        $\mathrm{col} \leftarrow \mathrm{col} + 1$\;
    }
}
\Return $\Amat{k}{\activeDoFs}{\L}$\;
\end{algorithm}}

The adaptive subdivision matrices enter the overall adaptive loop during the \emph{unrefinement} in the \textsc{Solve} step in Section~\ref{subsec:solve}. The unrefinement gathers the contributions of the FEEC basis functions $\freeQ{\FEECbasis}{k}{\L}{\fineIdxone}$ of the spaces $\FEECspace{k}{\L}$ to $\hierSDFspace{k}{\C}{\L}$ using the adaptive subdivision matrices $\Amat{k}{\activeDoFs}{\L}$, see Example~\ref{ex:matrix_assembly} and~\cite{Piel.2026} for more details.

\subsection{The adaptive loop}
\label{subsec:adaptive_loop}

This section describes the adaptive loop used in the numerical experiments. The loop consists of the steps
\begin{equation}
\begin{tikzcd}
    \textsc{Solve} \arrow[r] & \textsc{Estimate} \arrow[r] & \textsc{Mark} \arrow[r] & \textsc{Refine} \arrow[r] & \textsc{Pad} \arrow[llll, rounded corners=0pt, to path={(\tikztostart.south) -- ([yshift=-1.em]\tikztostart.south) -- ([yshift=-1.em]\tikztotarget.south) -- (\tikztotarget.south)}]
\end{tikzcd}
\end{equation}

and is initialized with a admissible domain sequence $\C^0$. Throughout the adaptive loop, the general picture of the computational objects in Section~\ref{subsec:computational_representations} stays the same, the loop only refines $\C$ iteratively, and with that the active DoFs $\activeDoFSet{k}{}(\C)$ and the adaptive subdivision $k$-form spaces $\hierSDFspace{k}{\C}{\L}$. The \textsc{Pad} step is necessary because the \textsc{Refine} step only guarantees $\C^{\mathrm{refine}} \in \allcoverings$, not $\C^{\mathrm{refine}} \in \allpartitions$, see Definition~\ref{def:subdiv_partition}. This means that the \textsc{Pad} step effectively restores linear independence and exactness of the complex.

\subsubsection{Solve}
\label{subsec:solve}

The details of the \textsc{Solve} step are typically problem-specific. The common thread for methods using subdivision differential forms is that the bilinear forms that constitute the weak formulation are first assembled using the spaces $\FEECspace{k}{\L}$ on $\T_\L$ and then unrefined to the spaces $\hierSDFspace{k}{\C}{\L}$ using the adaptive subdivision matrices $\Amat{k}{\C}{\L}$, see~\cite{Piel.2026}. The resulting linear system then needs to be solved by an appropriate solver. The following example spells out the single steps for the vector Laplace test case from Section~\ref{subsec:vector_laplace}.

\begin{example}
    \label{ex:matrix_assembly}
    This example describes the $\textsc{Solve}$ step for the vector Laplace equation from Section~\ref{subsec:vector_laplace}. We use a standard finite elements library like FEniCS \citep{Logg.2012} to assemble the $0$-form mass matrix $\mathbf{M}_\L$ and the gradient-coupling matrix $\mathbf{B}_\L$ in Equation~\eqref{eq:mixed_laplacian1} and the curl--curl matrix $\mathbf{C}_\L$ in Equation~\eqref{eq:mixed_laplacian2} over the FEEC spaces $\FEECspace{k}{\L}$.

    Unrefining these matrices using the adaptive subdivision $k$-form matrices $\Amat{k}{\C}{\L}$ yields the matrices
    \begin{equation}
        \label{eq:FE_matrix_assembly}
        \mathbf{M}_\C = (\Amat{0}{\activeDoFs}{\L})^\top \; \cdot \; \mathbf{M}_\L \; \cdot \; \Amat{0}{\activeDoFs}{\L},
        \quad
        \mathbf{C}_\C = (\Amat{1}{\activeDoFs}{\L})^\top \; \cdot \; \mathbf{C}_\L \; \cdot \; \Amat{1}{\activeDoFs}{\L},
        \quad
        \mathbf{B}_\C = (\Amat{0}{\activeDoFs}{\L})^\top \; \cdot \; \mathbf{B}_\L \; \cdot \; \Amat{1}{\activeDoFs}{\L}
    \end{equation}
    over the spaces $\hierSDFspace{k}{\C}{\L}$ or $\hierSDFspaceZero{k}{\C}{\L}$. The right-hand side of Equation~\eqref{eq:mixed_laplacian2} is $f_\C = (\Amat{1}{\C}{\L})^\top \cdot f_\L$.

The mixed vector Laplace problem can then be assembled into the block matrix system
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:mixed_block_system}
    \begin{bmatrix}
        -\mathbf{M}_\C & \mathbf{B}_\C \\
        \mathbf{B}_\C^\top & \mathbf{C}_\C
    \end{bmatrix} \; \cdot \; \begin{bmatrix}
        \sigma_\C \\
        u_\C
    \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}
        0 \\
        f_\C
    \end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}
We denote this block system matrix by $K_\C$. $K_\C$ is symmetric indefinite, so we use \emph{MINRES} with a block-diagonal SPD preconditioner
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:preconditioner}
    \mathbf{P} =
    \begin{pmatrix}
        \mathbf{M}_\C & 0 \\
        0 & \mathbf{S_\C}
    \end{pmatrix},
    \qquad
    \mathbf{S}_\C= \mathbf{C}_\C + \mathbf{B}_\C^\top\, \mathrm{diag}(\mathbf{M}_\C \mathbf{1})^{-1}\, \mathbf{B}_\C,
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{S}_\C$ approximates the Schur complement of the $(1,1)$ block by replacing $\mathbf{M}_\C^{-1}$ with a row-sum lumped inverse ($\mathbf{1}$ the all-ones vector of length $\vert \activeDoFSet{0}{} \vert$). A multilevel preconditioner (e.g. BPX or a subdivision-aware multigrid V-cycle) would be the natural choice to exploit the hierarchical structure of $\hierSDFspace{k}{\C}{\L}$, but constructing one for the subdivision basis is nontrivial. We instead use the simpler, level-agnostic block-diagonal preconditioner above. Section~\ref{subsec:vector_laplace} investigates the impact of that choice on the solver performance.
\end{example}

\subsubsection{Estimation}
\label{subsec:estimate}

We compute error indicators $\eta_{\l,\coarseIdxone}$ per face $\face{\l}{\coarseIdxone} \in \C$ of the domain sequence $\C$. The residual is evaluated on the finest level $\L$ and then aggregated to the coarse parent faces:
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:project_estimator_on_L_to_partition}
    \eta^2_{\l,\coarseIdxone} = \sum\limits_{\face{\L}{\fineIdxone} \; \in \; \Aop{}{\l}{\L}(\face{\l}{\coarseIdxone})} \eta^2_{\L,\fineIdxone},
\end{equation}
where $\eta^2_{\L,\fineIdxone}$ is evaluated in $\FEECspace{k}{\L}$. Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline_marking_a} shows an example domain sequence with residual $\eta^2_{\l,\coarseIdxone}$. Note the residuals are only computed for faces in the domain sequence and the interface faces are excluded to avoid overlap and double-counting. In the following, we will refer to $\eta^2$ and $\rho^2$ as the \emph{total} and \emph{refinable} residuals, respectively, defined as
\begin{equation}
    \label{def:total_and_refinable_residuals}
    \eta^2 = \sum\limits_{\l=\lzero}^{\L} \; \eta^2_\l \qquad \text{and} \qquad \rho^2 = \sum\limits_{\l=\lzero}^{\L-1} \; \eta^2_\l = \eta^2 - \eta^2_{\L} \qquad \text{with} \qquad \eta^2_{\l} = \sum\limits_{\face{\l}{\coarseIdxone} \; \in \; \F(\C_\l)} \eta^2_{\l,\coarseIdxone}.
\end{equation}
The refinable residual discards the contributions of faces $\face{\L}{\coarseIdxone}$ on level $\L$ of the admissible domain sequence $\C$. We measure this refinable residual because faces cannot be refined beyond level $\L$ without global mesh refinement to extend the mesh hierarchy. For this reason, the residual accumulated on level $\L$ can no longer be decreased. In fact, we find that
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:saturation_rate}
    \rho^2 / \eta^2 = 1 - \eta^2_\L / \eta^2 \eqqcolon \xi
\end{equation}
This \emph{saturation rate} $\xi \in [0,1]$ provides a good analytic tool and a concrete stopping criterion for the adaptive loop.

\begin{example}[Vector Laplace estimator]
For the mixed vector Laplace problem with $(\sigma_h, u_h) \in \hierSDFspace{0}{\C}{\L} \times \hierSDFspace{1}{\C}{\L}$ from Section~\ref{subsec:vector_laplace}, the basis functions are piecewise linear approximations of the subdivision limit functions. Writing $\iota \colon \hierSDFspace{k}{\C}{\L} \hookrightarrow \FEECspace{k}{\L}$ for the inclusion of the hierarchical space into the fine FEEC space, $\extd (\iota u_h)$ is piecewise constant in $\FEECspace{2}{\L}$ and $\boldsymbol{\delta}\extd (\iota u_h)$ vanishes elementwise, while its contribution appears in the curl jumps across edges. In $\FEECspace{2}{\L}$, the indicator takes the form
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    \eta_{\L,\fineIdxone}^2 = \quad &\sum\limits_{\face{\L}{\coarseIdxone} \,\in\, \F_\L} \!\! \robert{h_{\face{\L}{\fineIdxone}}^2} \big( \|f - \extd \sigma_h\|_{L^2(\face{\L}{\fineIdxone})}^2 + \|\sigma_h + \boldsymbol{\delta} u_h\|_{L^2(\face{\L}{\fineIdxone})}^2 \big) \\
    + &\sum\limits_{\edge{\L}{\fineIdxtwo} \, \in \, \E_\L} \robert{h_e} \big\|[\![\mathrm{curl}(u_h)]\!]\big\|_{L^2(\edge{\L}{\fineIdxtwo})}^2.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
The first sum contains the elementwise residuals of both equations, and the second sum contains the curl jump across interior edges $e$ of the fine mesh $\T_\L$. The per-face indicator $\eta_{\L,\fineIdxone}^2$ on level $\L$ can be translated back to the admissible domain sequence $\C$ using Equation~\eqref{eq:project_estimator_on_L_to_partition}.
\end{example}

\noindent We now have estimators $\eta_{\l,\coarseIdxone}$ per face of the admissible domain sequence $\C$. However, the atomic units of refinement in our scheme are vertex stars because they give rise to clean analysis. To enable vertex-based refinement for the adaptive algorithm, we need to sum up the face estimators $\eta_{\l,\coarseIdxone}$ to vertex estimators as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline_marking_b}. Recall from Section~\ref{subsec:selecting_active_dofs} the vertex equivalence classes $\vertclass{\coarseIdxtwo}$ from Equation~\eqref{eq:def_vertex_class} and their covering stars $\coveringStar(\vertclass{\coarseIdxtwo})$ from Equation~\eqref{eq:def_partition_star}, the active faces of $\C$ that touch the vertex. The vertex-based residual $\nu_\coarseIdxtwo$ our code uses is defined by
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:vertex_residual}
    \nu^2_\coarseIdxtwo = \tfrac{1}{a_\coarseIdxtwo} \; \sum\limits_{\face{\l}{\coarseIdxone} \; \in \; \coveringStar(\vertclass{\coarseIdxtwo}) \setminus \F_\L} \eta^2_{\l,\coarseIdxone}, \qquad \mathrm{where} \qquad a_\coarseIdxtwo = \sum\limits_{\face{\l}{\coarseIdxone} \; \in \; \coveringStar(\vertclass{\coarseIdxtwo}) \setminus \F_\L} \mathrm{area}(\face{\l}{\coarseIdxone}).
\end{equation}
Dividing by the cumulative area of the faces rescales the integral face estimator to a density per vertex. This rescaling makes the marking roughly level- and valence-agnostic. Otherwise, the contribution of coarse faces would be weighted higher and their adjacent vertices would be over-represented during the Dörfler marking step. Note also that we do not take faces on level $\L$ into account since they cannot be refined further, so their error is partically fixed.

\subsubsection{Marking}
\label{subsec:mark}

Our code marks vertices rather than faces. This enables us to use the quadrisection operator $\quadriV{\targetlevel}$ to refine single vertex stars, the central combinatorial object of the admissibility arguments in~\cite{companion_paper}. We rank the vertices by the vertex estimator $\nu_\coarseIdxtwo$, see Equation~\eqref{eq:vertex_residual} from Section~\ref{subsec:estimate}. For this purpose, we enumerate the $\vert \mathrm{V}_\C \vert$ vertex equivalence classes $\vertclass{\coarseIdxtwo}$ of $\C$ in order of decreasing residual, so that $\nu_{\coarseIdxtwo_1} \geq \nu_{\coarseIdxtwo_2} \geq \ldots \geq \nu_{\coarseIdxtwo_{\mathrm{V}-1}} \geq \nu_{\coarseIdxtwo_{\vert \mathrm{V}_\C \vert}}$.

The Dörfler marking algorithm\needcite{} builds the set $\mathrm{V}_\C^\mathrm{mark} \coloneq \{ \vertclass{\coarseIdxtwo_1}, \, \dots, \, \vertclass{\coarseIdxtwo_q} \}$ by greedily selecting the first $q$ vertex equivalence classes with the largest estimators such that the \emph{marked refinable residual}
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:marked_refinable_residual}
    \rho^2_{\R_\theta} \; \coloneqq \sum\limits_{\face{\l}{\coarseIdxone} \; \in \; \R_\theta} \eta^2_{\l, \coarseIdxone}, \qquad \text{where} \qquad \R_\theta = \bigcup\limits_{p=1}^q \coveringStar(\vertclass{\coarseIdxtwo_p}) \setminus \F(\C_\L),
\end{equation}
captures at least a fraction $\theta$ of the refinable residual $\rho$ of Equation~\eqref{def:total_and_refinable_residuals}, i.e.
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:doerfler_selection}
    \rho_{\R_\theta} \; \geq \; \theta \, \rho
\end{equation}
where $\theta \in (0, 1]$ is the Dörfler parameter. The set $\R_\theta \subset \F(\C_{<\L})$ collects all the faces that are refined during the \textsc{Refine} step, see Section~\ref{subsec:refine}; faces on level $\L$ are excluded, so $\rho_{\R_\theta}$ is a restriction of the refinable residual $\rho$ rather than of the total residual $\eta$.

\subsubsection{Refinement}
\label{subsec:refine}

The \textsc{Refine} step refines the marked faces $\face{\l}{\coarseIdxone} \in \R_\theta$ of the covering stars of the vertex equivalence classes $\vertclass{\coarseIdxtwo} \in \mathrm{V}_\C^{\mathrm{mark}}$ from Equation~\eqref{eq:marked_refinable_residual} as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline_marking_c}. Recall that the source level $\sourcelevel_\coarseIdxtwo$ and the target level $\targetlevel_\coarseIdxtwo$ measure the coarsest and finest levels at which a vertex of the vertex class $\vertclass{\coarseIdxtwo}$ occurs in the domain sequence $\C$, see Definition~\ref{def:vertex_classed}. Since we want to refine the faces around the vertex class by one level (unless $\targetlevel_\coarseIdxtwo = \L$), we compute the target level $\tilde{\targetlevel}_\coarseIdxtwo = \min(\targetlevel_{\coarseIdxtwo_p} \! + \! 1, \L)$ that is expected after refining around the vertex class $\vertclass{\coarseIdxtwo}$. The expected target levels $\tilde{\targetlevel}_\coarseIdxtwo$ must be computed on the fixed input admissible domain sequence $\C$ before any refinement takes place to avoid a cascade of ever increasing target levels. 

The \textsc{Refine} step homogenises the covering star of each marked vertex class $\vertclass{\coarseIdxtwo} \in \mathrm{V}_\C^{\mathrm{mark}}$ to one level beyond its target level $\targetlevel_\coarseIdxtwo$. Figure~\ref{fig:refine_interface} displays two examples of this refinement for vertex classes at an interface. Formally, we can express the refinement using the graded quadrisection $\quadriV{\tilde{\targetlevel}_\coarseIdxtwo}(\C, \vertclass{\coarseIdxtwo})$ from Equation~\eqref{eq:vertex_quadrisection}.

The result of the \textsc{Refine} step is the admissible domain sequence $\C^{\mathrm{refine}}$ given by
\begin{enumerate}[topsep=3pt, itemsep=-2pt]
    \item initialise the admissible domain sequence $\C^0 = \C$,
    \item refine covering stars iteratively for all $\vertclass{\coarseIdxtwo_p} \in \mathrm{V}_\C^{\mathrm{mark}}$ with $p = 1, \, \dots, \, \vert \mathrm{V}_\C^{\textrm{mark}} \vert$ to obtain
    \begin{equation}
        \C^{p} = \quadriV{\C^{p-1}}{\tilde{\targetlevel}_{\coarseIdxtwo}}(\vertclass{\coarseIdxtwo}),
    \end{equation}
    \vspace{-7mm}
    \item set $\C^{\mathrm{refine}} \coloneq \C^{\vert \mathrm{V}_\C^{\textrm{mark}} \vert}$.
\end{enumerate}
Because the expected target levels $\tilde{\targetlevel}_{\coarseIdxtwo}$ are computed on the fixed input domain sequence $\C$ and each quadrisection only ever homogenises a star upward to $\tilde{\targetlevel}_{\coarseIdxtwo}$, the order in which the $\vertclass{\coarseIdxtwo} \in \mathrm{V}_\C^{\mathrm{mark}}$ are processed does not affect the resulting partition $\C^p$. We may therefore apply them in any order, our code processes the coarsest vertex classed first for instance.

\subsubsection{Padding}
\label{subsec:padding}

The \textsc{Refine} step in the previous section produces the domain sequence $\C^{\mathrm{refine}}$. Crucially, $\C^{\mathrm{refine}}$ is not a admissible domain sequence because $\C^{\mathrm{refine}}$ might now contain \tobecut{pinch vertices or inadmissible faces} \guidance{inadmissible faces, pinch vertices, or boundary pinch vertices --- one for each clause of Definition~\ref{def:subdiv_partition}}. The \textsc{Pad} step repairs these defects and turns $\C^{\mathrm{refine}}$ in the admissible domain sequence $\C^{\mathrm{pad}}$. The goal is to keep the padded faces added during this process to a minimum to avoid inflating the number of DoFs in non-marked regions. The only exception is some grading that we apply around vertices whose covering star covers too many levels. This grading does not change the restored admissibility properties but improves the conditioning of the resulting matrix-vector system. This section covers all three kinds of padding one by one.

The \textsc{Pad} needs no machinery beyond the covering star $\coveringStar$ and the two quadrisection operators $\quadriF$ and $\quadriV{\targetlevel}$ of Section~\ref{subsec:subdiv_partition} since every padding substep is a quadrisection of either a covering star of a vertex class or of inadmissible faces.

\paragraph{Resolving vertex pinches.}

We iterate over all vertex classes $\vertclass{\coarseIdxtwo}$ of the refined admissible domain sequence to build the set of pinch vertices
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{V}_{\C^{\mathrm{refine}}}^{\mathrm{pinch}} = \{ \vertclass{\coarseIdxtwo} \in \mathrm{V}_{\C^{\mathrm{refine}}} \; \colon \; \vertclass{\coarseIdxtwo} \text{\; is a pinch vertex of } \C^{\mathrm{refine}}\}
\end{equation}
according to Definition~\ref{def:pinch_vertex}. \guidance{The test is applied at
\emph{every} level at which the vertex exists, not only at its target level
$\targetlevel_\coarseIdxtwo$, and a vertex on $\boundary\T_\l$ enters the set also when it is
a boundary pinch in the sense of Definition~\ref{def:boundary_pinch} --- that is, when one of
its refinement arcs fails to reach the domain boundary. Clause~(iii) of
Definition~\ref{def:subdiv_partition} needs no repair machinery of its own: an unanchored arc
is lifted by the very same graded quadrisection used below, because homogenising the whole
ring up to $\targetlevel_\coarseIdxtwo$ anchors the arc at both of its extremes.} The
admissible domain sequence $\C^{\mathrm{pinch}} \coloneq \C^{\vert \mathrm{V}_{\mathrm{pinch}} \vert}$ with all pinches resolved is obtained from the iteration
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:pinch_resolved_subdiv_partition}
    \tobecut{\C^p = \mathcal{Q}^{\star \to \targetlevel_{\coarseIdxtwo_p}}_{\vertclass{\coarseIdxtwo_p}}(\C^{p-1})}
    \guidance{\C^p = \quadriV{\targetlevel_{\coarseIdxtwo_p}} \big( \C^{p-1}, \, \vertclass{\coarseIdxtwo_p} \big)} \quad \text{for} \quad \vertclass{\coarseIdxtwo_p} \in \mathrm{V}_{\C^{\mathrm{refine}}}^{\mathrm{pinch}} \quad \text{with} \quad \; p \in \{1, \, \dots, \, \vert \mathrm{V}_{\C^{\mathrm{refine}}}^{\mathrm{pinch}} \vert \},
\end{equation}
and $\C^0 = \C^\mathrm{refine}$ the admissible domain sequence after the \textsc{Refine} step and $\targetlevel_{\coarseIdxtwo_p}$ the target level of $\vertclass{\coarseIdxtwo_p}$ in $\C^p$. In words, for each pinched vertex $\vertclass{\coarseIdxtwo} \in \mathrm{V}_\C$, we homogenise its covering star to its target level, lifting the coarse arcs to $\targetlevel_{\coarseIdxtwo_p}$ and merging them into a single disc. 

Every pinch vertex carries a cohomology defect. The severity of the defect depends on the concrete configuration of the pinch, see Definition~\ref{def:pinch_vertex} and Figure~\ref{fig:pinches}. Section~\ref{subsec:exactness_atomic} later confirms that resolving the coarse region around a pinch vertex successfully repairs these defects.

\paragraph{Resolving inadmissible faces.}

Similarly to resolving pinches, we iterate over all face $\face{\l}{\coarseIdxone}$ of the admissible domain sequence $\C^{\mathrm{pinch}}$ to build the set of inadmissible faces
\begin{equation}
    \F_{\mathrm{inadmissible}} = \big\{ \face{\l}{\coarseIdxone} \in \F(\C^{\mathrm{pinch}}) \; \colon \; \face{\l}{\coarseIdxone} \text{\; is not admissible} \big\}
\end{equation}
according to Definition~\ref{def:admissible_face}. The admissible domain sequence $\C^{\mathrm{admissible}}$ where all faces are admissible is given by
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:inadmissible_faces_resolved_subdiv_partition}
    \tobecut{\C^{\mathrm{admissible}} = \mathcal{Q}^{\triangle}_{\F_{\mathrm{inadmissible}}} (\C^{\mathrm{pinch}}).}
    \guidance{\C^{\mathrm{admissible}} = \quadriF \big( \C^{\mathrm{pinch}}, \, \F_{\mathrm{inadmissible}} \big).}
\end{equation}
In words, we refine every inadmissible face by one level because the refined faces are guaranteed to have an admissible vertex on level $\l+1$.

\paragraph{Grading covering stars.}

The grading is supposed to smooth out transitions across vertex classes $\vertclass{\coarseIdxtwo} \in \mathrm{V}_{\C^{\mathrm{admissible}}}$ with large level differences. Unlike the first two rules, the grading step does not restore exactness or linear independence but is intended to improve the conditioning of the matrix-vector system and to avoid numerical artifacts in these regions. The predicate to identify the vertex classes $\vertclass{\coarseIdxtwo}$ to grade is the \emph{level gap} $\mu_\coarseIdxtwo$ defined by
\begin{equation}
    \label{def:level_gap}
    \mu_\coarseIdxtwo \coloneq \targetlevel_\coarseIdxtwo - \sourcelevel_\coarseIdxtwo.
\end{equation}
The grading code iterates over the vertex classes $\vertclass{\coarseIdxtwo} \in \mathrm{V}_{\C^{\mathrm{admissible}}}$ and assembles the set
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{V}_{\C^{\mathrm{admissible}}}^{\mathrm{grading}} \coloneq \big\{ \vertclass{\coarseIdxtwo} \; \colon \; \mu_\coarseIdxtwo > 2 \big\}
\end{equation}
The cut-off value of $2$ works well in our simulations of the vector Laplace equation in Section~\ref{subsec:vector_laplace} but can generally be chosen arbitrarily.

The vertex classes marked for grading are refined using the quadrisection operator to obtain the graded admissible domain sequence $\C^{\mathrm{grading}} \coloneq \C^{\vert \mathrm{V}_{\mathrm{admissible}} \vert}$ as the result of the iterative construction
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:grading_subdiv_partition}
    \tobecut{\C^p = \mathcal{Q}^{\star \to \targetlevel_{\coarseIdxtwo_p} - 2}_{\vertclass{\coarseIdxtwo_q}}(\C^{p-1})}
    \guidance{\C^p = \quadriV{\targetlevel_{\coarseIdxtwo_p} - 2} \big( \C^{p-1}, \, \vertclass{\coarseIdxtwo_p} \big)} \quad \text{for} \quad \vertclass{\coarseIdxtwo_p} \in \mathrm{V}_{\C^{\mathrm{admissible}}}^{\mathrm{grading}} \quad \text{with} \quad \; p \in \{1, \, \dots, \, \vert \mathrm{V}_{\C^{\mathrm{admissible}}}^{\mathrm{grading}} \vert \}
\end{equation}
and $\C^0 = \C^{\mathrm{admissible}}$. Note the additional $-2$ in the quadrisection operator $\quadriV{\targetlevel_{\coarseIdxtwo_p} - 2}$. It ensures that the covering star $\coveringStarC{\C^p}(\vertclass{\coarseIdxtwo_p})$ on the graded partition $\C^p$ has a level gap of $2$ or less.

\paragraph{Summary.}

Every stage of the \textsc{Pad} step uses either the vertex-class based quadrisection operator $\quadriV{\targetlevel}$ or the face-based $\quadriF$.

The pinch padding resolves pinch vertices \guidance{and boundary pinch vertices, that is
clauses~(ii) and~(iii) of Definition~\ref{def:subdiv_partition},} and thus repairs cohomological artifacts and spurious harmonics, while the admissibility padding \guidance{enforces clause~(i) and} mostly restores linear independence of the active basis functions $\activeDoFSet{k}{\C}$. The grading only refines around vertex classes with large level jumps to improve the conditioning of the resulting linear system.

\guidance{The boundary clause is worth separating out because it is invisible under Dirichlet
boundary conditions: an unanchored refinement arc costs one spurious harmonic $1$-form under
natural boundary conditions only, so a domain sequence carrying the defect can pass every
Dirichlet-only check unchanged.}

Every rule only ever refines and adds a few faces that locally repair its violation, and no face is refined beyond level $\L$.

\begin{remark}[Padding order matters]
\label{rem:padding_order}
The three padding substeps do not commute: resolving a pinch vertex can lead to an admissible face becoming inadmissible (e.g. Figure~\ref{fig:two_padding_iterations}), which is why $\textsc{Pad}$ always runs pinch resolution before inadmissible-face padding, before grading.
\end{remark}

We do not \robert{(yet?)} have a proof that $\textsc{Pad}$ terminates. For our implementation, we wrapped the pinch resolution, face admissibility padding and the grading in a loop that terminates once a pass no longer refines any face, i.e., none of the predicates fires. A counter records the productive passes, which is $1$ for the large majority of test cases we ran, but is not always $1$: Figure~\ref{fig:two_padding_iterations} shows a small hand-constructed domain sequence that needs two passes to stabilise.

\section{Numerical Experiments}
\label{sec:numerical_experiments}

This section verifies the properties of the adaptive subdivision $k$-form spaces $\hierSDFspace{k}{\C}{\L}$ and $\hierSDFspaceZero{k}{\C}{\L}$ predicted in Section~\ref{sec:construction_of_hierarchical_subdiv_spaces} and tests the adaptive loop presented in Section~\ref{subsec:adaptive_loop}. Section~\ref{subsec:exactness_atomic} illustrates how domain sequences with pinch vertices or inadmissible faces can break the de~Rham complex and linear independence, and how padding repairs the domain sequences. Section~\ref{subsec:maxwell} then confirms on the Maxwell eigenvalue problem that the spurious modes are confined to the eigenvalue $0$, implying that $\extd \circ \extd = 0$ pointwise for every domain sequence. Section~\ref{subsec:vector_laplace} exercises the full adaptive loop on a vector Laplace problem with a singular solution. The emphasis throughout is on the admissibility of the adaptive subdivision $k$-form spaces, i.e. whether the active DoFs are linearly independent and preserve the cohomology of the de Rham complex, rather than on approximation rates.

Before applying the adaptive loop to a PDE, we validate the admissibility
construction directly on a suite of domain sequences that represent minimal test cases. This allows us to study the different modes of failure of adaptive subdivision $k$-form spaces and to confirm that padding repairs them correctly so that the assumptions of the Theorems~\ref{theorem:linear_independence} and~\ref{theorem:exactness} are satisfied again. Each domain sequence is a small concentric-ring mesh around a single, irregular vertex of chosen valence at the centre.

We want to verify the linear independence of all active DoFs $\activeDoFSet{k}{}(\C)$ of the respective domain sequences $\C$. We will test that by computing $\vert \activeDoFSet{k}{}(\C) \vert$ by counting elements and $\mathrm{dim}(\hierSDFspace{k}{\C}{\L}) \!=\! \mathrm{rank} (\Amat{k}{\C}{\L})$. If these two coincide, all active DoFs are indeed linearly independent.

Further, we want to test whether the adaptive $k$-form subdivision spaces $\hierSDFspace{k}{\C}{\L}$ possess the correct cohomology. Since the domains are topological disks, we expect the spaces of harmonic $k$-form to have the dimensions $\mathrm{dim}(\mathcal{H}^0) \!=\! 1$ and $\mathrm{dim}(\mathcal{H}^1) \!=\! \mathrm{dim}(\mathcal{H}^2) \!=\! 0$ for $\hierSDFspace{k}{\C}{\L}$ without boundary conditions. For each domain sequence and its respective adaptive subdivision $k$-form space, we compute $\mathrm{dim}(\mathcal{H}^k) \!=\! \mathrm{dim}(\mathrm{ker}(\extd^{k})) - \mathrm{rank}(\extd^{k-1})$ explicitly from the rank of the discrete $k$-form derivative $\extd^k$ before and after padding.

\smallskip

Figures~\ref{fig:pinch_resolution} and~\ref{fig:admissibility_padding} show the test domain sequences on the left and the padded domain sequences on the right, annotating every domain sequence with its active $k$-form DoF count $\lvert\activeDoFSet{k}{}\rvert$, the true space dimension $\dim V^k = \operatorname{rank}(\Aop{k}{\C}{\L})$, and the harmonic dimension $\dim(\mathcal{H}^k)$. Note that we denote $V^k = \hierSDFspace{k}{\C}{\L}$ here to save horizontal space. The reported data belongs to $\hierSDFspace{k}{\C}{\L}$ throughout because the geometric defects of the domain sequence are confined to the interior and thus, the vanishing-trace spaces $\hierSDFspaceZero{k}{\C}{\L}$ exhibit the same algebraic defects and are repaired identically by the padding. The sole exception is the domain sequence in Figure~\ref{fig:pinch_resolution_boundary_pinches} with boundary pinches for which we present data for $\hierSDFspaceZero{k}{\C}{\L}$.

\smallskip

We can identify two trends in the data that are consistent with the theoretical predictions. First, Figure~\ref{fig:pinch_resolution} shows that vertex pinches lead to spurious $1$-form harmonics but do not introduce any linear dependencies since $\vert \activeDoFSet{k}{} \vert = \mathrm{dim}(\hierSDFspace{k}{\C}{\L})$ in any case, before and after padding. We can observe that padding indeed removes these spurious $1$-form harmonics.

The individual panels show that the dimensions $\mathrm{dim}(\mathcal{H}^1)$ of the spurious harmonic $1$-form spaces increase with the level difference of the target level and the \guidance{coarsest} level of the faces \tobecut{in the covering star of} \guidance{incident to} the pinch vertex. For instance, the \tobecut{covering} \guidance{domain sequence} in Figure~\ref{fig:pinch_level_1} has a pinch on level $1$ with the \guidance{coarsest} level being $0$. This leads to $\mathrm{dim}(\mathcal{H}^1) \! = \! 1$. In contrast, Figure~\ref{fig:pinch_level_2} has its pinch on level $2$ and $\mathrm{dim}(\mathcal{H}^1) \! = \! 2$. \guidance{Figure~\ref{fig:pinch_mixed_level} also has a pinch on level $2$, but the faces between its two refinement arcs mix the levels $0$ and $1$. It is the coarsest level $0$ around the vertex that sets the dimension, not the second finest level $1$: we again observe $\mathrm{dim}(\mathcal{H}^1) \! = \! 2$, and not $1$.}

\guidance{\robert{This rule is empirical and rests on the three cases above.} It is stated
here as an observation, not a consequence of~\cite{companion_paper}, which establishes that a
pinch obstructs the exactness argument but explicitly does not claim the converse --- that
every pinch produces a defect of a predictable dimension. Worth re-checking if further pinch
configurations are added.}

The dimensions $\mathrm{dim}(\mathcal{H}^1)$ of the spurious harmonic $1$-form spaces also increase with the number $\gamma$ of fine arcs around the pinch, see Definition~\ref{def:pinch_vertex}. Figure~\ref{fig:pinch_level_1} has $\gamma = 2$ and $\mathrm{dim}(\mathcal{H}^1) = 1$, while Figure~\ref{fig:double_pinch} has $\gamma = 3$ and $\mathrm{dim}(\mathcal{H}^1) \!=\! 2$.

Figure~\ref{fig:pinch_resolution_boundary_pinches} presents data of the spaces $\hierSDFspaceZero{k}{\C}{\L}$ with vanishing boundary conditions, and thus we expect $\mathrm{dim}(\mathring{\mathcal{H}}^0) \!=\! \mathrm{dim}(\mathring{\mathcal{H}}^1) \!=\! 0$ and $\mathrm{dim}(\mathring{\mathcal{H}}^2) \!=\! 1$. The domain sequence in the figure has six pinches at the boundary. The table reports $\mathrm{dim}(\mathring{\mathcal{H}}^1) = 6$ before padding, implying that the spurious harmonics act additively for multiple pinches.

\smallskip

The second observation is concerned with inadmissible faces and the defects they cause. \guidance{The domain sequence in Figure~\ref{fig:coarse_ring} exhibits linear dependence in every form degree: the active $0$-form DoFs have $18$ linear dependencies, the $1$-form DoFs have $36$, and the $2$-form DoFs have $18$. The cohomology, on the other hand, is already correct on the input domain sequence, $\mathrm{dim}(\mathcal{H}^1) \!=\! 0$, so a closed coarse loop of inadmissible faces costs us linear independence without creating a spurious harmonic.} The padding step refines the entire coarse loop from level $0$ to level $1$.

\guidance{The domain sequence in Figure~\ref{fig:horsehoe} opens the loop, and the picture is the same: linear dependencies but no spurious harmonics. They split into $17$ $0$-form DoFs, $31$ $1$-form DoFs, and $15$ $2$-form DoFs.} The padding space removes these linearly dependent DoFs while maintaining the correct cohomology.

Finally, Figure~\ref{fig:nested_center} shows the prototypical configuration around a vertex that has been refined gradedly by the vertex quadrisection operator from Definition~\ref{def:vertex_quadrisection_operator}. We see that the domain sequence has no inadmissible faces since all vertices become interior at just the right level, see Figure~\ref{fig:covering_interior_vertices}. This means that we can run the \textsc{Pad} step unconditionally in the adaptive loop without any risks of over-refining regions that are already admissible.

\smallskip

Every domain sequence considered so far is a topological disk, so the only harmonic $1$-forms observed have been the defect-driven ones that padding removes. Figure~\ref{fig:genuine_cohomology} shows a domain sequence with a non-trivial topology (two holes). \guidance{The input domain sequence has $15$ linearly dependent $1$-form DoFs and $\mathrm{dim}(\mathcal{H}^1) \!=\! 4$. After padding, all DoFs are independent again and $\mathrm{dim}(\mathcal{H}^1) \!=\! 2$ exactly. The padding removed the $2$ spurious harmonics while leaving both genuine harmonics untouched.}

\smallskip

This section confirms that the \textsc{Pad} step correctly restores the geometric defects of the domain sequence produced by the \text{Refine} and makes it a admissible domain sequence. Thus, it also restores the important algebraic properties of linear independence and exactness of the complex of the adaptive subdivision $k$-form spaces, both with and without vanishing trace boundary conditions.

\subsection{Maxwell Eigenvalue problem}
\label{subsec:maxwell}

This section studies the Maxwell eigenvalue problem, a classical test case to verify the properties of discrete differential complexes. Section~\ref{subsec:exactness_atomic} has already established that the \textsc{Pad} step restores the correct cohomology for the spaces $\hierSDFspace{k}{\C}{\L}$ by repairing the geometric defects of the domain sequence $\C$ so that it becomes a admissible domain sequence. In constrast, this section studies how the spurious harmonic $1$-forms of a domain sequence concretely affect the solution of the eigenproblem.

The weak form of the Maxwell cavity (curl--curl) eigenvalue problem on the domain $\M = [0,\pi]^2$ with mesh $\T_\L$ is given by: Find $\lambda_{h,\coarseIdxone} \in \mathbb{R}$ and $u_\coarseIdxone \in \hierSDFspaceZero{1}{\C}{\L}$ such that
\begin{equation}
  (\extd u_i, \extd v)_\M = \lambda_{h,\coarseIdxone} (u_i, v)_\M \qquad \forall v \in \hierSDFspaceZero{1}{\C}{\L}
\end{equation}
with analytic spectrum $\lambda_\coarseIdxone = m^2 + n^2$, where $m$ and $n$ are integers. We will approximate the discrete spectra on the domain sequence $\C^{\mathrm{pinch}}$ with a pinch on level $\l=1$ as displayed in Figure~\ref{fig:Maxwell_inexact_DoFmesh} and its padded version, the admissible domain sequence $\C^{\mathrm{padded}}$ from Figure~\ref{fig:Maxwell_exact_DoFmesh}. Since the domain is contractible, its relative first Betti number is zero. Thus, $\hierSDFspaceZero{1}{\C^{\mathrm{padded}}}{\L}$ has no $1$-form harmonics because $\C^{\mathrm{padded}}$ is a admissible domain sequence. The pinch of $\C^{\mathrm{pinch}}$ introduces a one-dimensional spurious harmonic $1$-form space into $\hierSDFspaceZero{1}{\C^{\mathrm{pinch}}}{\L}$.

Applying Loop subdivision alters the domain slightly, so we do not compare the discrete spectrum directly with the analytic spectrum  $\lambda_\coarseIdxone$. Instead, we compare the discrete spectra $\lambda_\coarseIdxone$ with the spectrum of $\FEECspaceZero{k}{\L}$ with $\C^{\mathrm{FEEC}} = \T_\L$, a superspace of both $\hierSDFspaceZero{1}{\C^{\mathrm{pinch}}}{\L}$ and $\hierSDFspaceZero{1}{\C^{\mathrm{padded}}}{\L}$ that verifiably converges to the correct spectrum, see~\cite{Arnold.FEEC}.

Figure~\ref{fig:Maxwell_eigenvalues} reports the discrete spectra with $\lambda_{h,\coarseIdxone} > 0$ for all three domain sequences. Outside of small approximation errors, the spectra agree across all the three
partitions. That means that the defects caused by the spurious $1$-form harmonics of $\hierSDFspaceZero{1}{\C^{\mathrm{pinch}}}{\L}$ are confined to the eigenspace of $\lambda_{h,\coarseIdxone} = 0$. The reason for that behaviour is Theorem~\ref{theorem:differential_complex}, stating that $\extd \circ \extd \equiv 0$ holds pointwise on $\hierSDFspaceZero{0}{\C}{\L}$ for all domain sequences $\C \in \allcoverings$, regardless of whether they satisfy the admissible domain sequence conditions. This implies that $\extd u$ does not carry a spurious curl of any gradient, and thus the non-zero spectrum does not get polluted, cf.~\cite{Boffi.2010}.

The $1$-form Laplace problem is given by
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:strong_laplace}
    (\extd \boldsymbol{\delta} + \boldsymbol{\delta} \extd) u = f
\end{equation}
on a domain $\M$. Since the operator $\boldsymbol{\delta}$ is not well-defined for a (subdivision) $1$-form, we introduce the auxiliary variable $\boldsymbol{\delta} \sigma = u$ and use the FEEC-typical mixed weak formulation: Find $\sigma \in H^1(\M)$ and $u \in \HcurlM{\M}$ such that
\begin{align}
    \label{eq:mixed_laplacian1}
    (\sigma, \tau)_\M - (u, \extd \tau)_\M &= 0 \qquad \forall \tau \in H^1(\M), \\
    \label{eq:mixed_laplacian2}
    (\extd \sigma, v)_\M + (\extd u, \extd v)_\M &= (f, v)_\M \qquad \forall v \in \HcurlM{\M}.
\end{align}

This section studies the circular layer test case from \cite{Cabanas.2026} to exercise the entire adaptive loop of \textsc{Solve}--\textsc{Estimate}--\textsc{Mark}--\textsc{Refine}--\textsc{Pad}. Its analytical solution is given by
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:circular_layer_test_case}
    \Vec{u}_{\mathrm{CL}}(x) = \tanh \big(\alpha (\vvert{\vvert{x}}^2 - R^2) \big) \; \Vec{b}(x) \quad \text{with} \quad \Vec{b}(x) = \begin{bmatrix}
        2 x_2^2 (x_2 - 1)^2 (x_1^2 - x_1) (2 x_1 - 1) \\[2pt]
        2 x_1^2 (x_1 - 1)^2 (x_2^2 - x_2) (2 x_2 - 1)
    \end{bmatrix},
\end{equation}
where $\alpha = 100$, $R = 0.3$, and $\Vec{u}_{\mathrm{CL}}$ is the vector-proxy of the $1$-form $u_{\mathrm{CL}}$. The vector part $\Vec{b}(x)$ provides a background field while the $\tanh$ term adds a sharp, radially symmetric transition between $+\Vec{b}$ and $-\Vec{b}$ localised on the ring $\vvert{\vvert{x}} = R$. The right-hand side $f_{\mathrm{CL}}$ corresponding to $\Vec{u}_{\mathrm{CL}}$ can be computed using the (strong) Laplace equation~\eqref{eq:strong_laplace}.

\newcommand{\Ltwogap}{g}
Notably, Figure~\ref{fig:cl_l2_vs_dofs} shows that the converged $L^2$ error $\Ltwoerror^\laststep(\hierSDFspace{1}{\C}{\L})$ of the adaptive method does not coincide with the reference $L^2$ error $\Ltwoerror(\FEECspace{1}{\L})$. Table~\ref{tab:cl_headline} compares the final states of the adaptive method terminated at $\xi \leq 0.05$ and $\xi \leq 0.3$ with the FEEC reference. The tabulated $L^2$ errors allow us to compute the \emph{$L^2$ gap} $\Ltwogap(\L)$ by
\begin{equation}
    \Ltwogap(\L) \coloneq \Ltwoerror^\laststep(\hierSDFspace{1}{\C}{\L}) \; / \; \Ltwoerror(\FEECspace{1}{\L}).
\end{equation}
Table~\ref{tab:cl_headline} reveals that, as $\L$ and thus the DoF saving factor increase, so does the $L^2$ gap of the approximation errors. This is not a solver defect, but the price of saving DoFs. Marking in the energy norm concentrates refinement on the sharp $\tanh$ ring, where the residual is large, and leaves the smooth bulk coarse.

We verify this directly for $L=4$ by integrating $\vvert{u_h-u}^2$ against a sharp indicator function of width $w=0.05$ tracking the transition circle to obtain the ring error $r$. Integrating against the indicator's nodal complement in $\CG_1$ yields to the bulk error $b$. The two contributions add up exactly to the total $L^2$ error so that $\Ltwoerror^2 = r^2 + b^2$, for both the adaptive solution and the FEEC reference at the same $\L$. For $\L=4$, we measured
\begin{equation}
\begin{alignedat}{4}
    &r(\hierSDFspace{1}{\C}{4}) &&= 3.30\cdot10^{-4} \, , \qquad &&b(\hierSDFspace{1}{\C}{4}) &&= 3.93\cdot10^{-4} \, ,\\
    &r(\FEECspace{1}{4}) &&= 3.30\cdot10^{-4} \,, \qquad &&b(\FEECspace{1}{4}) &&= 1.69\cdot10^{-4}.
\end{alignedat}
\end{equation}
The ring error agrees closely between the two methods (differing by only $0.02\%$), while the bulk error differs substantially. This implies that the $L^2$ gap is a function of the bulk errors growing apart between the two methods, not an artifact of the ring. In this sense, the $L^2$ gap explicitly measures the loss of accuracy incurred by saving DoFs. Where that unavoidable gap concentrates is a choice, not a necessity. This is where reading the adaptive loop backwards, as \emph{greedy coarsening} of the fine reference space rather than incremental refinement of a coarse one, is useful: Dörfler marking greedily decides, step by step, which part of the fine space to keep active, based on the residual estimator. Because the sharp $\tanh$ ring dominates that estimator, coarsening is cheapest in estimator terms everywhere except the ring, so the bulk is coarsened away first and absorbs almost all of the $L^2$ gap.

The most relevant shortcoming of methods the adaptive subdivision $k$-form spaces is that saving DoFs does not translate to faster solves. Table~\ref{tab:solve_stats_L4} summarises the important metrics around the solve, including the wallclock time needed by the MINRES solver alongside the DoF count, the number of iterations, the condition number of the preconditioned system matrix $P^{-1}K_{\C^n}$, and the number of non-zero elements of $K_{\C^n}$ per DoF. Finally, we list the metrics for the fine FEEC reference $\FEECspace{k}{\L}$ with $\L=4$.

The wallclock times reveal that the solver performance degrades rapidly as the adaptive iterations increase. In fact, the FEEC reference method is about $130$ times faster than the adaptive solve of step $6$ even though it has roughly $4.7$ times as many DoFs. The reasons for that are found in the last three columns of Table~\ref{tab:solve_stats_L4}. First, the number of iterations needed for the MINRES solver to converge skyrocket initially until they start to become flat around step $4$. This happens due to the successive activation of basis functions across finer and finer levels, until at step $4$ the finest possible basis functions are first included in the adaptive space. This means that the condition number $\kappa(P^{-1}K_{\C^n})$ increases until then, explaining the increased number of iterations. This is a conditioning effect distinct from the rank-deficiency discussed in Section~\ref{subsec:unrefinement_and_solve}. $\C^n$ remains a admissible domain sequence throughout the loop, so $\Amat{k}{\activeDoFs}{\L}$ has full column rank at every step, yet $\kappa(P^{-1}K_{\C^n})$ still grows. The second reason for the large wallclock times is that the increased number of nonzero entries $\text{nnz}(K_{\C^n})/(\vert \activeDoFSet{0}{} \vert + \vert \activeDoFSet{1}{} \vert)$ per active DoF increases the number of operations per MINRES iteration. The increase of non-zero entries is due to the smoothing nature of subdivision, meaning that coarse basis functions have larger supports and thus cover a lot of triangles of $\T_\L$. The unrefinement step in Equation~\ref{eq:FE_matrix_assembly} extends this large number of non-zeros to $K_\C$.

\smallskip

The adaptive method thus only wins in DoFs and memory, not wall-clock, with the current solver. A truncated basis in the spirit of THB-splines \citep{Gianelli.2016} might be able to mitigate that problem. Truncating each coarse active basis function's representation over the region already covered by finer active DoFs would remove exactly the redundant coarse/fine support overlap responsible for the ill-conditioning and reduces the number of non-zeros, both without changing the discrete space itself. This means we would target both measured mechanisms directly, rather than working around them with a different solver. Truncating the subdivision-induced basis functions will be a subject of future work.

\section{Conclusion}

\robert{We have presented a complete matrix-level framework for hierarchical subdivision $k$-form spaces and their use in an adaptive loop for structure-preserving PDE discretisations. The central observation is that any active subdivision basis function is a column of an accumulated subdivision matrix $\Smat{k}{\l}{\L}$; selecting columns according to a admissible domain sequence produces a hierarchical space $\hierSDFspace{k}{\C}{\L}$ whose linear independence and de Rham exactness are guaranteed by the partition condition proved in the companion paper~\cite{companion_paper}. Once the hierarchical subdivision matrix $\Amat{k}{\activeDoFs}{\L}$ is assembled, all bilinear forms are obtained by Galerkin restriction of the embedding-level operators without recomputing any integrals, and the active-space linear system reduces to standard sparse linear algebra on the finest mesh $\T_\L$.

The adaptive loop adds a subdivision-specific \textsc{Pad} step to the standard \textsc{Solve}--\textsc{Estimate}--\textsc{Mark}--\textsc{Refine} cycle. Padding alternates pinch resolution with inadmissible-faces padding and grading to a fixed point: pinch resolution repairs a pinched coarse region that would otherwise carry a spurious harmonic (a closed but non-exact discrete $1$-form, breaking de Rham exactness), while inadmissible-faces padding and grading enforce the interior-vertex (Good-vertex) requirement and bound the level jump at coarse/fine interfaces. Both rules only ever refine, are local, and are inexpensive relative to the linear solve.

The numerical experiments confirm the two key structural properties of the framework. For the Maxwell eigenvalue problem, exact admissible domain sequences reproduce the reference FEEC spectrum without spurious modes; inexact ones introduce them. This serves as a direct diagnostic for the correctness of the active-DoF construction and the de Rham complex structure. For the mixed vector Laplace equation, the adaptive loop concentrates refinement at the location of steep gradients and achieves convergence behaviour consistent with the hierarchical approximation theory.

Several directions remain open. The precise correspondence between the subdivision PAD step and the $L$-chain completion used in hierarchical B-spline AFEM~\cite{Cabanas.2026} has not been established; clarifying this connection would relate the two frameworks and potentially enable convergence proofs to be transferred. On the solver side, the block-diagonal LDLT preconditioner used here is effective for the problem sizes considered, but a multigrid preconditioner that respects the larger-than-Whitney supports of the subdivision basis would reduce the per-step cost for larger problems; this requires a prolongation operator compatible with $\Amat{k}{\activeDoFs}{\L}$ and is left for future work. Finally, a promising direction is to adopt the overlapping patch framework of Bressan~\cite{Bressan.2024} in place of the non-overlapping admissible domain sequence used here. Overlapping patches can provide a richer set of admissible active-DoF configurations and may admit a more flexible PAD step, at the cost of requiring a partition-of-unity treatment to maintain linear independence; adapting the matrix-level assembly and the de Rham complex to that setting is an open problem.}

\guidance{This appendix gives the full active-DoF assembly routine
$\textsc{BuildActiveDoFs}$, whose role in
Section~\ref{sec:construction_of_hierarchical_subdiv_spaces} is described around
Equation~\eqref{eq:active_k_form_dofs_per_level}. It evaluates
Definition~\ref{def:active_k_form_dofs} directly, one level at a time. Each level needs three
sets and no state carried from any other level: the region $\M_\l$, its extension
$\extendedPatch{\M}_\l$, and the ancestor patch $\ancestorPatch{\M_{\l+1}}$ of the next finer
region. The selection $\mathring{\K}^k_\l(\cdot)$ is the membership test of
Lemma~\ref{lemma:open_dofs_membership}: a simplex qualifies exactly when every level-$\l$ face
containing it lies in the region, which is a single pass over the face-incidence relation.

The regions themselves are recovered downward from the leaf partition, using that
quadrisection assigns each level-$(\l\!+\!1)$ face the parent it was split from. Because the
levels are independent, the loop below may be run in any order, or in parallel.}

\guidance{\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{$\textsc{BuildActiveDoFs}(\domseq{\L})$: active degrees of freedom from a domain sequence}
\label{alg:buildactivedofs}
\BlankLine
\KwIn{domain sequence $\domseq{\L}$ given by its leaf partition, form degree $k$}
\KwOut{active DoFs $\activeDoFSet{k}{}(\domseq{\L})$, tagged by level}
\BlankLine
\tcp{1.\ Recover the regions downward from the leaf partition}
$\M_\L \leftarrow$ leaf faces at level $\L$\;
\For{$\l = \L - 1, \, \ldots, \, \lzero$}{
    $\M_\l \leftarrow \{\, \mathrm{parent}(f) \; : \; f \in \M_{\l+1} \,\} \; \cup \;$ leaf faces at level $\l$\;
}
\BlankLine
\tcp{2.\ One independent set difference per level}
$\activeDoFSet{k}{} \leftarrow \emptyset$\;
\For{$\l = \lzero, \, \ldots, \, \L$}{
    \tcp{(a) extension: every level-$\l$ face carrying a vertex of $\M_\l$}
    $\extendedPatch{\M}_\l \leftarrow \bigcup_{\vertex{\l}{\coarseIdxone} \in \V_\l(\M_\l)} \FEl{\l}(\vertex{\l}{\coarseIdxone})$\;
    \tcp{(b) removed region: the level-$\l$ faces refined beyond $\l$}
    $A_\l \leftarrow \ancestorPatch{\M_{\l+1}}$ \quad \tcp*{$A_\L = \emptyset$ by convention}
    \tcp{(c) select by Lemma~\ref{lemma:open_dofs_membership}: all containing faces inside}
    $I_\l \leftarrow \{ \simplex{k}{\l}{\coarseIdxone} \; : \; \F\K^k_\l(\simplex{k}{\l}{\coarseIdxone}) \subseteq \extendedPatch{\M}_\l \}$\;
    $R_\l \leftarrow \{ \simplex{k}{\l}{\coarseIdxone} \; : \; \F\K^k_\l(\simplex{k}{\l}{\coarseIdxone}) \subseteq A_\l \}$\;
    $\activeDoFSet{k}{} \leftarrow \activeDoFSet{k}{} \; \cup \; \big( I_\l \setminus R_\l \big)$\;
}
\Return $\activeDoFSet{k}{}$\;
\end{algorithm}}
